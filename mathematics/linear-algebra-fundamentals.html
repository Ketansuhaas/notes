Rank, subspaces, and projections

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran · 28 October 2026

Rank

$\operatorname{rank}(A) = \dim C(A) = \dim C(A^T)$ = # independent columns = # independent rows = # pivots.

Intuition: the number of genuinely independent directions $A$ can produce.

Example

$$ A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \text{col}_2 = 2\,\text{col}_1 \;\Rightarrow\; \operatorname{rank}(A) = 1. $$

Solving $Ax = b$

Behaviour depends on shape and rank $r$ of $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$.

Tall ($m > n$), full column rank $r = n$: $N(A) = \{0\}$, so at most one solution.

Example

$$ A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} \Rightarrow x = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} \ \text{(unique, } b \in C(A)\text{)} $$

$$ b = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} \notin C(A) \;\Rightarrow\; \text{no exact solution — least squares instead.} $$

Wide ($m < n$), full row rank $r = m$: $C(A) = \mathbb{R}^m$, so always consistent; $n - r$ free variables $\Rightarrow$ infinitely many solutions.

Example

$$ A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; x = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}}_{x_p} + t\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix}}_{\in\, N(A)}, \ t \in \mathbb{R}. $$

Square ($m = n$): full rank $\Rightarrow$ invertible, unique $x = A^{-1}b$ for every $b$. Rank-deficient $\Rightarrow$ no solution or infinitely many, depending on $b$.

Example

$$ A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\ (\operatorname{rank} 1): \quad b = \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix} \in C(A) \Rightarrow \text{infinitely many}, \qquad b = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} \notin C(A) \Rightarrow \text{none.} $$

Vector space, subspace

Vector space $V$: closed under addition and scalar multiplication, $0 \in V$. Subspace $S \subseteq V$: a subset that is itself a vector space.

Intuition: a subspace is a flat slice of the space that passes through the origin.

Example

$$ L = \{\, t(1,1) : t \in \mathbb{R} \,\} \subset \mathbb{R}^2 \ \text{is a subspace (line through origin)}. $$

$$ M = \{\, (1,0) + t(0,1) : t \in \mathbb{R} \,\} \subset \mathbb{R}^2 \ \text{is not (} 0 \notin M \text{)}. $$

Fundamental subspaces

For $A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$, $\operatorname{rank}(A) = r$:

  • Column space $C(A) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$, $\dim = r$
  • Null space $N(A) = \{x : Ax = 0\} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, $\dim = n - r$
  • Row space $C(A^T) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, $\dim = r$
  • Left null space $N(A^T) \subseteq \mathbb{R}^m$, $\dim = m - r$

Intuition: $C(A)$ is everywhere $Ax$ can reach; $N(A)$ is everything that collapses to $0$.

$$ N(A) \perp C(A^T) \ \text{in } \mathbb{R}^n, \qquad N(A^T) \perp C(A) \ \text{in } \mathbb{R}^m. $$

Example

Using $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ ($m=2, n=3, r=2$):

$$ C(A) = \mathbb{R}^2, \quad N(A) = \operatorname{span}\begin{pmatrix}-1\\-1\\1\end{pmatrix}, \quad C(A^T) = \operatorname{span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}\right\}, \quad N(A^T) = \{0\}. $$

Check: $(-1,-1,1) \cdot (1,0,1) = 0$ and $(-1,-1,1) \cdot (0,1,1) = 0$ — $N(A) \perp C(A^T)$. ✓

Projection, via the residual

Intuition: the closest point in a subspace is the one whose leftover error is perpendicular to it.

Onto a line $\operatorname{span}(a)$: write $p = \hat x\, a$, residual $e = b - p$. Require $e \perp a$:

$$ a^T(b - \hat x a) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat x = \frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta} \;\Rightarrow\; p = \frac{a a^T}{a^Ta}\, b =: Pb. $$

Example

$$ a = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\ b = \begin{pmatrix}4\\0\end{pmatrix} \Rightarrow \hat x = \frac{4}{2}=2,\ p=\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix},\ e=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-2\end{pmatrix},\ a\cdot e = 0.\ \checkmark $$

Onto $C(A)$: write $p = A\hat x$, residual $e = b - A\hat x$. Require $e \perp C(A)$, i.e. $A^Te = 0$:

$$ A^T(b - A\hat x) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; A^TA\,\hat x = A^Tb \;\Rightarrow\; \hat x = (A^TA)^{-1}A^Tb \;\Rightarrow\; p = \underbrace{A(A^TA)^{-1}A^T}_{P}\,b. $$

Example

Reusing the tall $A$ and its inconsistent $b=(1,1,3)$ from above:

$$ A^TA = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}, \quad A^Tb = \begin{pmatrix}4\\4\end{pmatrix} \;\Rightarrow\; \hat x = \begin{pmatrix}4/3\\4/3\end{pmatrix}, \quad p = \begin{pmatrix}4/3\\4/3\\8/3\end{pmatrix}, \quad e = \begin{pmatrix}-1/3\\-1/3\\1/3\end{pmatrix}. $$

Check $A^Te = 0$: $\ -\tfrac13+0+\tfrac13=0$ and $0-\tfrac13+\tfrac13=0$. ✓

The big picture

$A$ is a map taking a vector from one space ($\mathbb{R}^n$) to another ($\mathbb{R}^m$). It's constrained both ways: it can't reach every point of the output space, and it can't keep every input alive — some vectors just die to $0$.

$$ \mathbb{R}^m = C(A) \oplus N(A^T), \qquad \mathbb{R}^n = C(A^T) \oplus N(A). $$

Output side. Every $b$ splits uniquely into a reachable part and a part $A$ is blind to:

$$ b = b_{\text{col}} + b_\perp, \quad b_{\text{col}} \in C(A), \ b_\perp \in N(A^T). $$

$Ax$ can only ever land in $C(A)$, so $b_\perp$ can never be reached — the best we can do is $Ax = b_{\text{col}}$, exactly the projection above.

Intuition: $b_\perp$ is the part of $b$ that $A$ is structurally blind to.

Example

Reusing $b = p + e$ from the projection above:

$$ \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\3\end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}4/3\\4/3\\8/3\end{pmatrix}}_{b_{\text{col}} = p\, \in\, C(A)} + \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}-1/3\\-1/3\\1/3\end{pmatrix}}_{b_\perp = e\, \in\, N(A^T)}. $$

Input side. Every $x$ splits uniquely too:

$$ x = x_{\text{row}} + x_{\text{null}}, \quad x_{\text{row}} \in C(A^T), \ x_{\text{null}} \in N(A). $$

$$ Ax = A(x_{\text{row}} + x_{\text{null}}) = Ax_{\text{row}} + \underbrace{Ax_{\text{null}}}_{=\,0} = Ax_{\text{row}}. $$

The null-space part of $x$ is invisible to $A$ — only $x_{\text{row}}$ ever matters.

Intuition: $A$ only ever "sees" the row-space part of its input.

Example

Using the wide $A$ and $x=(1,1,0)$ from the solving example above ($Ax = (1,1)$):

$$ x = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1/3\\1/3\\2/3\end{pmatrix}}_{x_{\text{row}}} + \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}2/3\\2/3\\-2/3\end{pmatrix}}_{x_{\text{null}}}, \qquad Ax_{\text{row}} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = Ax. $$

($x_{\text{row}}$ is also the minimum-norm solution to $Ax = (1,1)$.)

Together: $A$ is a bijection from the row space onto the column space, with $N(A)$ and $N(A^T)$ as the blind spots on either side.
