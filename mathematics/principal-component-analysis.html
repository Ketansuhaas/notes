Principal component analysis

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran · 29 October 2026

Setup: variance along a direction

Given centered data points $x_1,\dots,x_n\in\mathbb{R}^d$ (mean already subtracted), stacked as rows of $X$, the sample covariance matrix is $C=\tfrac1n\sum_ix_ix_i^T=\tfrac1nX^TX$, symmetric and positive semidefinite.

Projecting each point onto a unit direction $v$ gives a scalar $x_i^Tv$; the variance of these projections is:

$$ \operatorname{Var}(v) = \tfrac1n\sum_i (x_i^Tv)^2 = v^TCv. $$

Intuition: $C$ packages how the data spreads out in every direction at once; $v^TCv$ reads off the spread along one particular direction $v$.

Example

Reusing $C=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ (our running matrix, eigenvalues $3,1$, eigenvectors $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1), \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$): along $v=(1,0)$, $v^TCv=2$. Along $v=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$: $Cv=3v$, so $v^TCv=3$, more spread.

Derivation 1: maximize variance directly

The first principal component is the unit direction capturing the most variance:

$$ v_1 = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\|v\|=1} v^TCv. $$

By the spectral theorem, $C=Q\Lambda Q^T$ with $\lambda_1\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_d\geq0$. Write $v=\sum_ic_iq_i$ in the eigenbasis, with $\sum_ic_i^2=1$ since $v$ is a unit vector:

$$ v^TCv = \sum_i\lambda_ic_i^2 \ \leq\ \lambda_1\sum_ic_i^2 = \lambda_1, $$

with equality exactly when $v=q_1$, the top eigenvector.

Intuition: a unit vector's variance is a weighted average of the eigenvalues; to make that average as large as possible, put all the weight on the biggest one.

Example

With $C$ above, every unit $v$ has $v^TCv=3c_1^2+c_2^2\leq3$, maximized at $c_1=1,c_2=0$, i.e. $v=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$: the same direction found by hand.

Derivation 2: constrained optimization

Same objective, via the Lagrangian tangency condition (from The Lagrangian and KKT conditions): maximize $f(v)=v^TCv$ subject to $g(v)=v^Tv-1=0$.

$$ \nabla f(v) = 2Cv, \quad \nabla g(v) = 2v, \quad \nabla f = \lambda\nabla g \ \ \Rightarrow\ \ Cv = \lambda v. $$

Stationary points are exactly the unit eigenvectors of $C$, and at a stationary point $v^TCv=\lambda v^Tv=\lambda$: the objective value is the eigenvalue itself, so maximizing picks out the largest one.

Intuition: "maximize variance on the unit sphere" and "find eigenvectors of $C$" are the same question; constrained optimization and the eigenvalue problem share their stationary points.

Example

$Cv=\lambda v$ gives $\lambda=3,v=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ or $\lambda=1,v=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$: the two stationary points, with $\lambda=3$ the maximum, matching Derivation 1 exactly.

Further components

The second component maximizes the same variance subject to the extra constraint $v\perp v_1$; the same argument, restricted to that orthogonal complement, gives the next eigenvector $v_2$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_2$. Repeating gives all $d$ components: the eigenvectors of $C$, ranked by eigenvalue.

Intuition: each new component grabs the most remaining variance, in a direction already perpendicular to everything claimed so far.

Connection to the SVD

If $X=U\Sigma V^T$ (from Singular value decomposition), then:

$$ C = \tfrac1nX^TX = \tfrac1nV\Sigma^2V^T. $$

So the principal components are exactly the columns of $V$ (the right singular vectors of $X$), and the variance captured by component $i$ is $\sigma_i^2/n$.

Intuition: PCA is the spectral theorem applied to $C$, which is secretly the SVD of $X$; nothing new, just a direct application of what's already there.

Dimensionality reduction

Projecting onto the top $k$ components, $Z=XV_k\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}$, is exactly the rank-$k$ truncation from Low-rank approximation: it keeps the directions of largest variance and discards the rest, the best possible rank-$k$ summary of the data.

Intuition: PCA compresses data by keeping only the few directions that matter most, the same "keep the big singular values, drop the small ones" idea as SVD.
