Rank, subspaces, and projections

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran

Builds on: Four views of matrix multiplication, Lines, planes, and spheres in $\mathbb{R}^n$ · Leads to: Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and defectiveness, Singular value decomposition

$A$ maps $\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$. Four subspaces say what it reaches, what it kills, and how close it gets otherwise.

Subspaces, span, basis

A subspace is a flat slice through the origin. A span is everywhere some vectors can reach; a basis reaches it with no redundancy.

Subspaceclosed under $+$ and scaling, contains $0$
Span$\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_k)=\{c_1v_1+\cdots+c_kv_k\}$
Basisindependent spanning set (drop one and the span shrinks)
Dimensionsize of any basis

Why "any" basis. Proof idea: if $k$ vectors span a space, any $k+1$ vectors in it are dependent ($k+1$ unknown weights, only $k$ coordinates to cancel, so a nonzero solution exists). So a basis can't be bigger than another basis, and by symmetry all bases have the same size. A full proof does the "exchange" one vector at a time.

Example

$(1,0,0),(0,1,0),(1,1,0)$ all have third entry $0$, and any $(a,b,0)=a(1,0,0)+b(0,1,0)$, so they span the $xy$-plane. They are dependent:

$$(1,1,0)=(1,0,0)+(0,1,0).$$

Any two are independent (neither is a multiple of the other) and still span, so any two form a basis and $\dim=2$.

Rank

How many independent directions $A$ can output. $Ax$ mixes columns (view 2), so rank counts the genuinely different columns.

$$\operatorname{rank}A:=\dim C(A)=\dim C(A^T)=\#\text{pivots}.$$

Step 1: an $r$-dim bottleneck. Let $r=\dim C(A)$ and put a basis of $C(A)$ into the columns of $B$. Each column $a_j$ lies in $C(A)$, so it is a mix of $B$'s columns, $a_j=Bc_j$. Stack the recipes $c_j$ as columns of $C$:

$$\underbrace{A}_{m\times n}=\underbrace{B}_{m\times r}\underbrace{C}_{r\times n}.$$

Step 2: row rank $\le$ column rank. By view 3, each row of $A$ is a mix of the $r$ rows of $C$:

$$\text{row}_i(A)=\text{row}_i(B)\,C \ \Rightarrow\ C(A^T)\subseteq\text{span of } r \text{ rows} \ \Rightarrow\ \dim C(A^T)\le\dim C(A).$$

Step 3: equality. Step 2 holds for every matrix, so apply it to $A^T$ too:

$$\dim C(A)=\dim C\big((A^T)^T\big)\le\dim C(A^T) \ \Rightarrow\ \dim C(A^T)=\dim C(A).$$

Step 4: pivots. Row operations replace rows by invertible mixes of rows, so they keep the row space, and they keep the solutions of $Ax=0$. Row-reduce (permuting columns if needed):

$$R=\begin{pmatrix}I_r&F\\0&0\end{pmatrix}:$$ $$I_r=\text{pivot columns},\ \ F=\text{free columns as mixes of pivots},\ \ 0=\text{dependent rows}.$$

The nonzero rows of $R$ are independent (the $I_r$ block), so $\dim C(A^T)=\dim C(R^T)=r=\#$pivots.

Example: rank 1 = one column times one row

Column 2 is $2\times$ column 1, so $C(A)$ has basis $B=(1,2,3)^T$, and the recipes are $a_1=1\cdot B$, $a_2=2\cdot B$, giving $C=(1\ \ 2)$:

$$\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\\3&6\end{pmatrix}}_{3\times2}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}}_{3\times1}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&2\end{pmatrix}}_{1\times2}.$$

Row reduction: $R_2-2R_1$ and $R_3-3R_1$ give $R=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&2\\0&0\\0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$, one pivot, $F=(2)$. Every row is a multiple of $(1,2)$, so row rank is also $1$. ✓

The four fundamental subspaces

$C(A)$: everywhere $Ax$ can reach. $N(A)$: everything $A$ crushes to $0$. Each has a perpendicular partner from $A^T$.

Column space $C(A)$in $\mathbb{R}^m$$\dim r$
Null space $N(A)=\{x:Ax=0\}$in $\mathbb{R}^n$$\dim n-r$
Row space $C(A^T)$in $\mathbb{R}^n$$\dim r$
Left null space $N(A^T)$in $\mathbb{R}^m$$\dim m-r$

The two "$\dim r$" entries are the previous section. The null space count comes from $R$, which has the same null space as $A$. Split $x$ into pivot and free parts:

$$Rx=\begin{pmatrix}I_r&F\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{\text{piv}}\\x_{\text{free}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{\text{piv}}+Fx_{\text{free}}\\0\end{pmatrix}=0 \iff x_{\text{piv}}=-Fx_{\text{free}}.$$

The $n-r$ free entries can be anything and then fix the rest:

$$N(A)=\Big\{\begin{pmatrix}-F\\I_{n-r}\end{pmatrix}x_{\text{free}}\Big\} \ \Rightarrow\ \dim N(A)=n-r,$$

since the $I_{n-r}$ block makes those $n-r$ columns independent. This is rank-nullity, $r+\dim N(A)=n$. The same count for $A^T$ ($m$ columns, rank $r$) gives $\dim N(A^T)=m-r$.

Perpendicular pairs. Take $x\in N(A)$ and any row-space vector $A^Ty$:

$$(A^Ty)^Tx=y^T(Ax)=y^T0=0 \ \Rightarrow\ N(A)\perp C(A^T).$$

Swap $A\leftrightarrow A^T$ for $N(A^T)\perp C(A)$.

Example: the rank-1 matrix above ($m=3$, $n=2$, $r=1$)

$C(A)=\operatorname{span}(1,2,3)$ and $C(A^T)=\operatorname{span}(1,2)$ (every row is a multiple of $(1,2)$). From $R$:

$$N(A):\ x_1+2x_2=0 \ \Rightarrow\ x=x_2\begin{pmatrix}-2\\1\end{pmatrix} \ \Rightarrow\ N(A)=\operatorname{span}(2,-1),\ \dim 1=n-r.$$

$$N(A^T):\ A^Ty=\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\2&4&6\end{pmatrix}y=0 \iff y_1+2y_2+3y_3=0,$$

a plane, $\dim 2=m-r$. Check $(1,2)\cdot(2,-1)=0$. ✓

Solving $Ax=b$

Solvable iff $b\in C(A)$. Unique iff $N(A)=\{0\}$. Shape and rank decide which.

Tall, $m\gt n$, $r=n$$R=\begin{pmatrix}I_n\\0\end{pmatrix}$$N(A)=\{0\}$: at most one solution
Wide, $m\lt n$, $r=m$$R=\begin{pmatrix}I_m&F\end{pmatrix}$$C(A)=\mathbb{R}^m$: always solvable, $n-m$ free variables
Square, $r=n$$R=I_n$unique $x=A^{-1}b$
Rank-deficientzero rows and $F$none or infinitely many

Why the table. All solutions are one solution plus the null space, by linearity:

$$Ax_p=b \ \Rightarrow\ \big(A(x_p+z)=b \iff Az=0\big) \ \Rightarrow\ \{\text{solutions}\}=x_p+N(A).$$

Then read off the dimension counts:

$$r=n \Rightarrow \dim N(A)=0 \Rightarrow \text{at most one solution};$$

$$r=m \Rightarrow \dim C(A)=m \Rightarrow C(A)=\mathbb{R}^m \Rightarrow \text{always solvable};$$

square with $r=n=m$: both, so exactly one solution, $x=A^{-1}b$. Rank-deficient ($r\lt m$ and $r\lt n$): $b$ may miss $C(A)$ (none), and if it hits, $N(A)\neq\{0\}$ adds a whole line or more (infinitely many).

Example: rank-1 $A$ above

Every row of $Ax=b$ is a multiple of $x_1+2x_2$:

$$Ax=\begin{pmatrix}x_1+2x_2\\2(x_1+2x_2)\\3(x_1+2x_2)\end{pmatrix}.$$

$b=(1,2,3)$: all three rows say $x_1+2x_2=1$. One solution is $x_p=(1,0)$, so $x=(1,0)+t(2,-1)$, infinitely many. $b=(1,0,0)$: row 1 says $x_1+2x_2=1$, row 2 says $2(x_1+2x_2)=0$, a contradiction, so no solution.

Projection

If $b$ is out of reach, take the closest reachable point: the one whose error is perpendicular to everything reachable.

Step 1: perpendicular error means closest. Suppose $e=b-A\hat x\perp C(A)$. Any other reachable point is $Ax$, and $A\hat x-Ax=A(\hat x-x)\in C(A)$ is $\perp e$, so Pythagoras gives

$$\|b-Ax\|^2=\|e+A(\hat x-x)\|^2=\|e\|^2+\|A(\hat x-x)\|^2\ \ge\ \|e\|^2.$$

Step 2: the normal equations. $e\perp C(A)$ means $e$ is $\perp$ every column $a_j$, i.e. $a_j^Te=0$ for all $j$. Stack those as rows:

$$A^Te=0 \iff A^T(b-A\hat x)=0 \iff A^TA\,\hat x=A^Tb.$$

Step 3: solve. With independent columns ($A$ tall, rank $n$), $A^TA$ is invertible (the exercise), so

$$\hat x=\underbrace{(A^TA)^{-1}A^T}_{n\times m}\,\underbrace{b}_{m\times1}, \qquad p=A\hat x=Pb,$$

$$P=\underbrace{A}_{m\times n}\underbrace{(A^TA)^{-1}}_{n\times n}\underbrace{A^T}_{n\times m}\quad(m\times m).$$

$P$ has rank $n$: $C(P)\subseteq C(A)$ (it starts with $A$), and $PA=A(A^TA)^{-1}(A^TA)=A$ puts every column of $A$ in $C(P)$. Projecting twice changes nothing, $P^2=A(A^TA)^{-1}(A^TA)(A^TA)^{-1}A^T=P$.

Onto a single line $a$ ($n=1$): $A^TA=a^Ta$ is a number, so its inverse is $1/(a^Ta)$:

$$\hat x=\frac{a^Tb}{a^Ta}, \qquad P=\frac{aa^T}{a^Ta}.$$

Example

$a=(1,1)$, $b=(2,0)$: $a^Tb=2$, $a^Ta=2$, so $\hat x=\tfrac22=1$, $p=1\cdot a=(1,1)$, $e=b-p=(1,-1)$, and $e\cdot a=1-1=0$. ✓

The big picture

$A$ sees only the row-space part of its input and reaches only the column space. The null spaces are its blind spots.

$$\mathbb{R}^n=C(A^T)\oplus N(A), \qquad \mathbb{R}^m=C(A)\oplus N(A^T).$$

Why every $x$ splits. Project $x$ onto the row space (previous section) and call the leftover $x_{\text{null}}$:

$$x=x_{\text{row}}+x_{\text{null}}, \qquad x_{\text{null}}\perp C(A^T).$$

$x_{\text{null}}$ is $\perp$ every row of $A$, so every entry of $Ax_{\text{null}}$ is $0$: it really is in $N(A)$. The split is unique because a vector in both spaces is $\perp$ itself, $v^Tv=0\Rightarrow v=0$. Dimensions agree: $r+(n-r)=n$. The same argument in $\mathbb{R}^m$ gives the second split.

$$Ax=A(x_{\text{row}}+x_{\text{null}})=Ax_{\text{row}}, \qquad b=\underbrace{b_{\text{col}}}_{\text{reachable}}+\underbrace{b_\perp}_{\text{never reached}}.$$

Row space to column space is one-to-one. If $Ax_{\text{row}}=Ax'_{\text{row}}$ then $x_{\text{row}}-x'_{\text{row}}$ is in the row space and in $N(A)$, so it is $0$. Least squares lands on $p=A\hat x$, and $b-p\perp C(A)$, so $p=b_{\text{col}}$: it solves $Ax=b_{\text{col}}$.

Example: rank-1 $A$, $x=(1,0)$

Project onto the row space $\operatorname{span}(1,2)$ with the line formula:

$$x_{\text{row}}=\frac{(1,2)\cdot(1,0)}{(1,2)\cdot(1,2)}(1,2)=\tfrac15(1,2),$$

$$x_{\text{null}}=(1,0)-\tfrac15(1,2)=\big(\tfrac45,-\tfrac25\big)=\tfrac25(2,-1)\in N(A).$$

$$Ax_{\text{row}}=\tfrac15\begin{pmatrix}1+4\\2+8\\3+12\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{pmatrix}=\text{col}_1(A)=Ax. \ \checkmark$$

Derive it yourself

Prove $N(A^TA)=N(A)$. Conclude that $A^TA$ is invertible exactly when $A$ has independent columns.

Solution

($\subseteq$) $Ax=0\Rightarrow A^TAx=A^T0=0$. ($\supseteq$) Multiply $A^TAx=0$ on the left by $x^T$:

$$0=x^TA^TAx=(Ax)^T(Ax)=\|Ax\|^2 \ \Rightarrow\ Ax=0.$$

Same null space. A square matrix is invertible iff its null space is $\{0\}$ (rank-nullity: $\dim N=0\iff$ rank $n$), so

$$A^TA \text{ invertible} \iff N(A^TA)=\{0\} \iff N(A)=\{0\} \iff \operatorname{rank}A=n.$$
