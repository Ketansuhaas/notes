Builds on: The covariance matrix, intuitively, The Lagrangian and KKT conditions, Singular value decomposition · Leads to: Variance decomposition and the eigenbasis

Describe each point with $k$ numbers instead of $d$, keeping as much spread as possible. Answer: the cloud's top $k$ axes.

The best direction is the top eigenvector

Find the ruler with the widest shadows. Spread along any direction is a weighted average of eigenvalues, so put all the weight on the biggest.

$$\underbrace{X}_{n\times d}\ \text{centered rows}, \quad \underbrace{C}_{d\times d}=\tfrac1nX^TX, \qquad v_1=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{\|v\|=1}v^TCv.$$

$C=\tfrac1nX^TX$ is the covariance $\tfrac1n\sum_ix_ix_i^T$: by the outer-product view of matrix multiplication, $X^TX$ is the sum over $i$ of (column $i$ of $X^T$)(row $i$ of $X$) $=x_ix_i^T$. And $v^TCv$ is the spread of the shadows on $v$. Two ways to maximize it:

Derivation 1: eigenbasis. Write $v=\sum_ic_iq_i$ in $C$'s orthonormal eigenvectors ($\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots$), so $\sum_ic_i^2=\|v\|^2=1$:

$$v^TCv=\sum_i\lambda_ic_i^2\ \le\ \lambda_1\sum_ic_i^2=\lambda_1 \quad(\text{each }\lambda_i\le\lambda_1).$$

Equality needs all weight on $\lambda_1$: $c=(1,0,\dots)$, i.e. $v=q_1$, where $q_1^TCq_1=\lambda_1$.

Derivation 2: Lagrangian. Maximize $v^TCv$ subject to $v^Tv=1$ (Lagrangian). For symmetric $C$, $\nabla_v(v^TCv)=2Cv$ and $\nabla_v(v^Tv)=2v$:

$$\nabla_v\big[v^TCv-\lambda(v^Tv-1)\big]=2Cv-2\lambda v=0 \ \Rightarrow\ Cv=\lambda v.$$

So every stationary point is an eigenvector, with objective $v^TCv=v^T(\lambda v)=\lambda$. The best is the largest $\lambda$.

Next component. Add $v\perp q_1$. Then $c_1=q_1^Tv=0$, and the same bound runs over $i\ge2$:

$$v^TCv=\sum_{i\ge2}\lambda_ic_i^2\le\lambda_2, \quad\text{equality at } v=q_2.$$

And so on: components = eigenvectors of $C$, ranked by $\lambda$.

Example

$C=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$: $\det(C-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)^2-1=0$ gives $\lambda=3,1$; solving $(C-\lambda I)v=0$ gives the stationary points $q_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ with value $3$, $q_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$ with value $1$. PC1 $=q_1$.

Compute it with the SVD

Don't form $C$ (that squares the condition number). The SVD of $X$ hands you the components directly.

Substitute $X=U\Sigma V^T$ and use $U^TU=I$:

$$C=\tfrac1nX^TX=\tfrac1nV\Sigma^TU^TU\Sigma V^T=V\Big(\tfrac1n\Sigma^T\Sigma\Big)V^T,$$

$$\Sigma^T\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^2,\dots,\sigma_d^2).$$

That is a spectral decomposition of $C$ ($V$ orthogonal, middle diagonal), so

$$\text{components}=\text{columns of }V, \qquad \lambda_i=\frac{\sigma_i^2}{n}.$$

Why not form $C$: the condition number (largest over smallest stretch) of $X^TX$ is $\sigma_1^2/\sigma_d^2=(\sigma_1/\sigma_d)^2$, the square of $X$'s, so rounding errors in the small directions get amplified that much more.

Example

Running $X$ (below): $X^TX=nC=4C=\begin{pmatrix}8&4\\4&8\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues $12,4$, so $\sigma_1=2\sqrt3$, $\sigma_2=2$ and $\lambda_i=\sigma_i^2/4=3,\ 1$. ✓

Compress, then reconstruct

Compress: each point's coordinates along the top $k$ axes. Decompress: map back; the dropped directions return as zero.

$$\underbrace{Z}_{n\times k}=\underbrace{X}_{n\times d}\underbrace{V_k}_{d\times k}, \qquad \underbrace{\hat X}_{n\times d}=\underbrace{Z}_{n\times k}\underbrace{V_k^T}_{k\times d}=X\underbrace{V_kV_k^T}_{\text{projection}}.$$

Row by row ($V_k$ has columns $q_1,\dots,q_k$): compress takes coordinates, reconstruct recombines them,

$$z_i=\big(q_1^Tx_i,\ \dots,\ q_k^Tx_i\big), \qquad \hat x_i=\sum_{j\le k}(q_j^Tx_i)\,q_j.$$

$\hat x_i$ is the orthogonal projection of $x_i$ onto $\operatorname{span}(q_1,\dots,q_k)$. $\hat X=ZV_k^T$ factors through a width-$k$ middle, so its rank is $\le k$, and storing $Z$ ($nk$ numbers) and $V_k$ ($kd$) replaces $nd$. In practice: center first; standardize if features have different units.

Kept vs. lost variance

Every unit of spread is either kept or lost. So "keep the most variance" and "reconstruct best" are the same goal.

$$\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^d\lambda_i}_{\text{total}}=\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{i\le k}\lambda_i}_{\text{kept}=\text{spread of }Z}+\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{i\gt k}\lambda_i}_{\text{lost}=\frac1n\sum_i\|x_i-\hat x_i\|^2}$$

$$\text{explained}(k)=\frac{\sum_{i\le k}\lambda_i}{\sum_i\lambda_i}.$$

Split each point. In the orthonormal basis $q_1,\dots,q_d$, the squared length is the sum of squared coordinates (Pythagoras), and the coordinates split into kept and dropped:

$$\|x_i\|^2=\underbrace{\sum_{j\le k}(q_j^Tx_i)^2}_{\|z_i\|^2}+\underbrace{\sum_{j\gt k}(q_j^Tx_i)^2}_{\|x_i-\hat x_i\|^2}.$$

Average over points. Each average square is a spread along an axis, $\tfrac1n\sum_i(q_j^Tx_i)^2=q_j^TCq_j=\lambda_j$, and the total is $\tfrac1n\sum_i\|x_i\|^2=\operatorname{tr}C=\sum_j\lambda_j$ (covariance note). That is the box above. The total is fixed by the data, so maximizing kept $=$ minimizing lost.

No other $k$-dimensional subspace keeps more. Take any orthonormal $W$ ($d\times k$). Its kept spread, with $C=\sum_i\lambda_iq_iq_i^T$:

$$\operatorname{tr}(W^TCW)=\sum_i\lambda_i\,\|W^Tq_i\|^2=\sum_i\lambda_iw_i, \qquad w_i:=\|W^Tq_i\|^2.$$

Each $w_i\in[0,1]$ (a projection can't lengthen the unit $q_i$) and $\sum_iw_i=\|W^TQ\|_F^2=\|W\|_F^2=k$. Weights capped at $1$ with total $k$ score highest when they sit on the $k$ largest $\lambda$'s: $\sum_i\lambda_iw_i\le\sum_{i\le k}\lambda_i$, reached by $W=V_k$.

Bonus: the new features are uncorrelated. Using $C=Q\Lambda Q^T$ and $Q^TV_k=$ the first $k$ columns of $I$:

$$\tfrac1nZ^TZ=V_k^T\big(\tfrac1nX^TX\big)V_k=V_k^TQ\,\Lambda\,Q^TV_k=\Lambda_k=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_k).$$

Example: $2\to1$

The covariance note's four points, $k=1$, $v_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$:

$$ \underbrace{X}_{4\times2}=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt3&\sqrt3\\-\sqrt3&-\sqrt3\\1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}, \quad \underbrace{Z}_{4\times1}=Xv_1=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt6\\-\sqrt6\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}, \quad \underbrace{\hat X}_{4\times2}=Zv_1^T=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt3&\sqrt3\\-\sqrt3&-\sqrt3\\0&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}. $$

Row 1 of $Z$: $\tfrac{\sqrt3+\sqrt3}{\sqrt2}=\tfrac{2\sqrt3}{\sqrt2}=\sqrt6$; row 3: $\tfrac{1-1}{\sqrt2}=0$. Row 1 of $\hat X$: $\sqrt6\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)=\sqrt3(1,1)$. Residuals: $x_1-\hat x_1=0$, $x_3-\hat x_3=(1,-1)$ with $\|\cdot\|^2=2$.

lost$\tfrac14(0+0+2+2)=1=\lambda_2$
explained$\tfrac3{3+1}=75\%$ ✓

Derive it yourself

Show the mean squared reconstruction error equals the dropped eigenvalues: $\tfrac1n\sum_i\|x_i-\hat x_i\|^2=\sum_{j\gt k}\lambda_j$.

Solution

Expand $x_i=\sum_j(q_j^Tx_i)\,q_j$; then $x_i-\hat x_i=\sum_{j\gt k}(q_j^Tx_i)\,q_j$. Orthonormal $q_j$: $\|x_i-\hat x_i\|^2=\sum_{j\gt k}(q_j^Tx_i)^2$. Average over $i$ and swap the sums: $\sum_{j\gt k}\tfrac1n\sum_i(q_j^Tx_i)^2=\sum_{j\gt k}q_j^TCq_j=\sum_{j\gt k}\lambda_j$. Example: $\lambda_2=1$. ✓
