Estimators and bias

An estimator turns a random sample into a guess, so the guess is random too. Unbiased means right on average, not right every time.

$$\hat\theta=\hat\theta(x_1,\dots,x_n), \qquad \text{unbiased: } \mathbb{E}[\hat\theta]=\theta, \qquad \text{bias}=\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta]-\theta.$$

Example

$\bar x_n$ is unbiased for $\mu$: $\mathbb{E}[\bar x_n]=\mu$.

Dividing by $n$ is biased low

$\bar x_n$ is fit to the very same data, so the points sit a little too close to it. The spread looks smaller than it is.

$$S_n^2=\frac1n\sum_i(x_i-\bar x_n)^2: \qquad \mathbb{E}[S_n^2]=\frac{n-1}n\sigma^2\lt\sigma^2.$$

$$\text{Fix: } \hat\sigma^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum_i(x_i-\bar x_n)^2, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\hat\sigma^2]=\sigma^2.$$

Proof. Expand the square and use $\sum_ix_i=n\bar x_n$:

$$\sum_i(x_i-\bar x_n)^2=\sum_ix_i^2-n\bar x_n^2.$$

Then $\mathbb{E}[X^2]=\operatorname{Var}X+(\mathbb{E}X)^2$ on each piece:

$$\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_i(x_i-\bar x_n)^2\Big]=n(\sigma^2+\mu^2)-n\Big(\frac{\sigma^2}n+\mu^2\Big)=(n-1)\sigma^2.$$

Divide by $n$: biased. Divide by $n-1$: exactly $\sigma^2$. ✓

Example: $n=2$

$\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2=\tfrac12(x_1-x_2)^2$, and $\mathbb{E}[(x_1-x_2)^2]=2\sigma^2$. So the sum averages $\sigma^2$: $S_2^2$ guesses only $\sigma^2/2$.

Degrees of freedom, geometrically

Residuals from $\bar x_n$ always sum to zero, so only $n-1$ of them are free. Estimating the mean spent one dimension.

$$e_i=x_i-\bar x_n: \quad \sum_ie_i=0 \ \text{always}, \qquad \text{but} \quad \sum_i(x_i-\mu)=n(\bar x_n-\mu)\ne0.$$

$\bar x_n$ is the projection of the data vector onto the all-ones direction. The residual is perpendicular to it.

$$\bar x_n=(\mathbb 1^T\mathbb 1)^{-1}\mathbb 1^Tx, \qquad \underbrace{x}_{n\times1}=\underbrace{\bar x_n\mathbb 1}_{\text{1 dim}}+\underbrace{e}_{n-1\text{ dims}}, \qquad e\perp\mathbb 1.$$

Example: $x=(1,2,6)$

$\bar x=3$, $e=(-2,-1,3)$, $\sum e_i=0$ ✓. $\sum e_i^2=14$: $S^2=14/3$, $\hat\sigma^2=14/2=7$.

Derive it yourself

Show $\sum_i(x_i-\mu)^2=\sum_i(x_i-\bar x_n)^2+n(\bar x_n-\mu)^2$ (Pythagoras), then take expectations to get $(n-1)\sigma^2$ again.

Solution

Write $x_i-\mu=(x_i-\bar x_n)+(\bar x_n-\mu)$ and square. The cross term is $2(\bar x_n-\mu)\sum_i(x_i-\bar x_n)=0$. Taking $\mathbb{E}$: $n\sigma^2=\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_i(x_i-\bar x_n)^2\big]+n\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}n$, so $\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_i(x_i-\bar x_n)^2\big]=(n-1)\sigma^2$.
