Taylor expansion in many variables

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran
calculustaylorgradientshessianoptimization

Builds on: Gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians, Quadratic forms · Leads to: Convexity and Jensen, Gradient descent on a quadratic

Zoom in close enough and any smooth function looks like a tilted plane (first order), then like a bowl or saddle (second order). Most "to first order" arguments in papers are this.

One variable

$$f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)\,h+\tfrac12f''(x)\,h^2, \qquad e^{h}\approx1+h+\tfrac12h^2.$$

Many variables: walk along a line

Reduce to one variable: walk from $x$ in direction $\Delta$, $\ g(t)=f(x+t\Delta)$, and apply the 1-D formula at $t=1$.

$$ g'(0)=\nabla f^T\Delta, \quad g''(0)=\Delta^TH\Delta \quad\Rightarrow\quad f(x+\Delta)\approx\underbrace{f(x)}_{1\times1}+\underbrace{\nabla f^T}_{1\times n}\underbrace{\Delta}_{n\times1}+\tfrac12\underbrace{\Delta^T}_{1\times n}\underbrace{H}_{n\times n}\underbrace{\Delta}_{n\times1}. $$

Example

$f=x_1^2+x_1x_2+x_2^2$ at $(1,1)$: $f=3$, $\nabla f=(3,3)$, $H=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Step $\Delta=(0.1,0)$:

$$3+0.3+\tfrac12(0.02)=3.31=f(1.1,1). \ \checkmark \ \ (\text{exact, since } f \text{ is quadratic})$$

What each term tells you

$\nabla f^T\Delta$slope: step $\Delta=-\eta\nabla f$ lowers $f$ by $\eta\|\nabla f\|^2$gradient descent
$\tfrac12\Delta^TH\Delta$curvature: bowl, dome, or saddlestep size, stability
bothminimize the bowl: $\nabla f+H\Delta=0$Newton: $\Delta=-H^{-1}\nabla f$

Derive it yourself

Take one Newton step from $(1,1)$ on the $f$ above. Where do you land, and why exactly there?

Solution

$H\Delta=-\nabla f=-(3,3)$. Since $H(1,1)^T=(3,3)$, $\Delta=-(1,1)$: you land at $(0,0)$, the minimum. For a quadratic the bowl model is $f$, so one Newton step is exact.
