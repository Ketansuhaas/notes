Taylor expansion in many variables

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran
calculustaylorgradientshessianoptimization

Builds on: Gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians, Quadratic forms · Leads to: Convexity and Jensen, Gradient descent on a quadratic

Zoom in close enough and any smooth function looks like a tilted plane (first order), then like a bowl or saddle (second order). Most "to first order" arguments in papers are this.

One variable

Find the parabola in $h$ that has the same value, slope and curvature as $f$ at $x$. That parabola is the second-order Taylor model.

Matching derivatives. Try $p(h)=a+bh+ch^2$ and demand it agree with $f(x+h)$ to second order at $h=0$:

$$p(0)=a=f(x), \qquad p'(0)=b=f'(x), \qquad p''(0)=2c=f''(x).$$

$$\Rightarrow\quad f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)\,h+\tfrac12f''(x)\,h^2.$$

The $\tfrac12$ is there because $\frac{d^2}{dh^2}h^2=2$.

Why it is accurate (repeated integration). The fundamental theorem of calculus, used twice:

$$f(x+h)=f(x)+\int_0^hf'(x+s)\,ds, \qquad f'(x+s)=f'(x)+\int_0^sf''(x+u)\,du.$$

Substitute the second into the first; the $f'(x)$ part integrates to $f'(x)h$:

$$f(x+h)=f(x)+f'(x)\,h+\underbrace{\int_0^h\!\!\int_0^sf''(x+u)\,du\,ds}_{R}.$$

This is exact. The region $0\le u\le s\le h$ is a triangle of area $\tfrac12h^2$ (for $h\gt0$), so $R$ is $\tfrac12h^2$ times an average of $f''$ over $[x,x+h]$. Since $f''$ is continuous, that average is a value $f''(\xi)$:

$$\boxed{\ f(x+h)=f(x)+f'(x)\,h+\tfrac12f''(\xi)\,h^2,\quad \xi\text{ between }x,\,x+h\ }$$

Replacing $f''(\xi)$ by $f''(x)$ errs by $\tfrac12|f''(\xi)-f''(x)|h^2$, which is $O(h^3)$ when $f'''$ is bounded (the mean value theorem gives $|f''(\xi)-f''(x)|\le\max|f'''|\cdot h$).

Example

$f=e^x$ at $x=0$: every derivative is $e^0=1$, so

$$e^{h}\approx1+1\cdot h+\tfrac12\cdot1\cdot h^2=1+h+\tfrac12h^2.$$

At $h=0.1$: $1.105$ versus $e^{0.1}=1.10517$; the gap $0.00017\approx h^3/6$ is the next term.

Many variables: walk along a line

Reduce to one variable: walk from $x$ in direction $\Delta$, $\ g(t)=f(x+t\Delta)$, and apply the 1-D formula at $t=1$.

The slope and curvature of $f$ along a line were derived in Gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians:

$$g'(t)=\nabla f(x+t\Delta)^T\Delta, \qquad g''(t)=\Delta^TH(x+t\Delta)\,\Delta.$$

Apply the 1-D formula to $g$ with base $0$ and step $1$, then set $g(1)=f(x+\Delta)$, $g(0)=f(x)$:

$$g(1)\approx g(0)+g'(0)\cdot1+\tfrac12g''(0)\cdot1^2$$

$$ \Rightarrow\quad f(x+\Delta)\approx\underbrace{f(x)}_{1\times1}+\underbrace{\nabla f^T}_{1\times n}\underbrace{\Delta}_{n\times1}+\tfrac12\underbrace{\Delta^T}_{1\times n}\underbrace{H}_{n\times n}\underbrace{\Delta}_{n\times1}. $$

The exact 1-D version carries over the same way: $f(x+\Delta)=f(x)+\nabla f^T\Delta+\tfrac12\Delta^TH(z)\Delta$ for some $z=x+\tau\Delta$ on the segment, $\tau\in(0,1)$.

Example

$f=x_1^2+x_1x_2+x_2^2$ at $(1,1)$: $f=1+1+1=3$, $\nabla f=(3,3)$, $H=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ (all from the gradients note). Step $\Delta=(0.1,0)$:

$$\nabla f^T\Delta=3(0.1)+3(0)=0.3, \qquad \Delta^TH\Delta=H_{11}(0.1)^2=0.02.$$

$$f(x+\Delta)\approx3+0.3+\tfrac12(0.02)=3.31.$$

Exact: $f(1.1,1)=1.21+1.1+1=3.31$. ✓ Along this line $g(t)=f(1+t,1)=(1+t)^2+(1+t)+1=t^2+3t+3$ is itself a parabola ($g'''=0$, so $R$ has no error), which is why the model is exact for a quadratic $f$.

What each term tells you

Read off what each term does to a step: the linear term picks the downhill direction, the quadratic term says how far you can go, and minimizing both together gives Newton's step.

Slope term. Put $\Delta=-\eta\nabla f$ into the linear part:

$$\nabla f^T(-\eta\nabla f)=-\eta\|\nabla f\|^2\lt0,$$

so a small step against the gradient lowers $f$ by about $\eta\|\nabla f\|^2$. That is gradient descent.

Curvature term. At a point where $\nabla f=0$ the model is $f(x)+\tfrac12\Delta^TH\Delta$, so the sign of $\Delta^TH\Delta$ (quadratic forms) decides: all positive = bowl (minimum), all negative = dome, mixed = saddle. Away from such points it limits the step size (see gradient descent on a quadratic).

Both: Newton. Minimize the model $m(\Delta)=f+\nabla f^T\Delta+\tfrac12\Delta^TH\Delta$ over $\Delta$. Its partial in $\Delta_i$, using $H$ symmetric:

$$\frac{\partial m}{\partial\Delta_i}=(\nabla f)_i+\tfrac12\Big(\sum_kH_{ik}\Delta_k+\sum_j\Delta_jH_{ji}\Big)=(\nabla f+H\Delta)_i.$$

Set it to zero (for $H$ positive definite this is the bottom of the bowl):

$$\nabla f+H\Delta=0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta_{\text{Newton}}=-H^{-1}\nabla f.$$

Derive it yourself

Take one Newton step from $(1,1)$ on the $f$ above. Where do you land, and why exactly there?

Solution

Solve $H\Delta=-\nabla f$:

$$\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Delta_1\\\Delta_2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-3\\-3\end{pmatrix}.$$

Subtract the rows: $\Delta_1-\Delta_2=0$. Then $3\Delta_1=-3$, so $\Delta=(-1,-1)$ and you land at $(1,1)+(-1,-1)=(0,0)$.

Why exactly there: $f$ is quadratic, so the model $m$ is $f$ (the remainder vanishes, $g'''=0$ on every line). Newton minimizes $m$, hence minimizes $f$. Check: $\nabla f(0,0)=(0,0)$, $f(0,0)=0$, and $H$ is positive definite, so this is the global minimum.
