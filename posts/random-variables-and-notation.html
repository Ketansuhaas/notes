Probability notation and random variables

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran · 30 October 2026
probabilitydeep-learning

Random variable vs. realization

A random variable $X$ is a map from a sample space $\Omega$ (outcomes of some random experiment) to a value space, usually $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{R}^d$. A realization $x$ is one particular output: $X$ is the machine, $x$ is one thing it produced.

Intuition: capital $X$ is "a number not yet seen"; lowercase $x$ is "a number already seen." Every formula below is really a statement about $X$, instantiated at a specific $x$.

Example

Flip a coin: $\Omega=\{H,T\}$, $X(H)=1$, $X(T)=0$. $X$ is the variable; $x=1$ is what happened on a given flip.

Events vs. values: $P(X=x)$ vs. $p(x)$

$P(\cdot)$ always eats an event, a subset of outcomes, and returns a probability: a number in $[0,1]$. $\{X=x\}$ and $\{X\in A\}$ are events, so $P(X=x)$ and $P(X\in A)$ are well-formed; $P(x)$ on its own is not, since $x$ is a number, not an event.

$p(\cdot)$, lowercase, is a different object: the probability mass function (pmf, discrete $X$) or probability density function (pdf, continuous $X$), a function of a value:

$$ \text{discrete: } p(x) := P(X=x), \qquad \text{continuous: } P(X\in A) = \int_A p(x)\,dx. $$

In the discrete case $p(x)$ is itself a probability, $\sum_x p(x)=1$. In the continuous case $p(x)$ is a density, not a probability: it can exceed 1, only $\int p(x)\,dx=1$ is required, and in fact $P(X=x)=0$ for every single point $x$ (a single point has zero width to integrate over). What a continuous density gives you is relative likelihood: $p(x)\,dx\approx P(X\in[x,x+dx])$ for small $dx$.

Intuition: $P$ answers "how likely is this set of outcomes," $p$ answers "how concentrated is the distribution right here." Why a single point can carry zero probability yet a nonzero density is really a question about measure, not probability, so it gets its own treatment in Lebesgue measure and KL divergence — for now, take the two displayed definitions as the working rules.

Example

Fair coin: $p(1)=p(0)=\tfrac12$, each a genuine probability. Standard uniform $X\sim\text{Unif}[0,2]$: $p(x)=\tfrac12$ for $x\in[0,2]$, a density, and $P(X=1)=0$ even though $p(1)=\tfrac12>0$.

Random vectors: joint, marginal, conditional

Everything above extends to a random vector $X=(X_1,\dots,X_d)\in\mathbb{R}^d$, with a joint density $p(x)=p(x_1,\dots,x_d)$ over all $d$ coordinates at once.

Shapes

$$ \underbrace{x}_{d\times1}=(x_1,\dots,x_d), \qquad \underbrace{p(x)}_{\text{scalar}}=\underbrace{p(x_1,\dots,x_d)}_{\text{one joint density, }d\text{ arguments}}. $$

Intuition: $p$ takes the whole vector at once and hands back a single number; it is one function of $d$ variables, not $d$ separate functions.

Three operations connect a joint to smaller pieces:

$$ \text{marginal: } p(x_1)=\sum_{x_2}p(x_1,x_2) \text{ or } \int p(x_1,x_2)\,dx_2, \qquad \text{conditional: } p(y\mid x)=\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)}, \qquad \text{independence: } p(x,y)=p(x)\,p(y). $$

Intuition: marginalizing sums (or integrates) a variable away because you no longer care what value it took; conditioning restricts to a slice where $x$ is fixed and renormalizes; independence is the special case where conditioning on $x$ changes nothing about $y$.

Repeated conditioning gives the chain rule, true for any ordering of the variables:

$$ p(x_1,\dots,x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i\mid x_1,\dots,x_{i-1}). $$

Intuition: generate the coordinates one at a time, each conditioned on everything generated so far; multiplying those one-at-a-time probabilities together rebuilds the full joint. This is the exact mechanism behind autoregressive models.

Example

Two coin flips, independent: $p(x_1,x_2)=p(x_1)p(x_2)=\tfrac14$ for every outcome. If instead $x_2$ always copies $x_1$: $p(x_2\mid x_1)=1$ when $x_2=x_1$ and $0$ otherwise, so $p(x_1,x_2)=p(x_1)p(x_2\mid x_1)=\tfrac12$ exactly when $x_1=x_2$, and $0$ otherwise — no independence, but the chain rule factorization still holds.

Expectation notation

$\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f(x)]$ averages $f$ over $x$ drawn from $p$:

$$ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f(x)] = \sum_x p(x)f(x) \quad \text{or} \quad \int p(x)f(x)\,dx. $$

Intuition: the subscript under $\mathbb{E}$ is not decoration, it names which distribution the randomness runs under. The moment two or more distributions are in play (true for essentially every post after this one, where $p_{\text{data}}$ and a model distribution both appear), dropping the subscript makes an expression ambiguous.

The data-generating distribution

Machine learning starts from a dataset, $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^n$ or $\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, and the standard working assumption is that these are i.i.d. (independent, identically distributed) samples from some fixed but unknown true distribution:

$$ x_i \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} p_{\text{data}}(x), \qquad \text{or} \qquad (x_i,y_i)\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} p_{\text{data}}(x,y). $$

$p_{\text{data}}$ is never directly observed, only sampled from, finitely many times. Nearly every method in this category of notes is best understood as trying to characterize, or approximate, some piece of $p_{\text{data}}$ using only that finite sample: a classifier approximates $p_{\text{data}}(y\mid x)$, a generative model approximates $p_{\text{data}}(x)$ itself.

Intuition: $p_{\text{data}}$ is the thing you actually want; the dataset is just a flashlight shining a few samples' worth of light on it. Common distributions next looks at concrete shapes $p_{\text{data}}$ or a model might take; Lebesgue measure and KL divergence makes the $P$ vs. $p$ distinction above fully rigorous and introduces the tool (KL divergence) used throughout the rest of this category to measure how close an approximation is to $p_{\text{data}}$.
