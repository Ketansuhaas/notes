Matrix calculus with differentials

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran
calculusmatrix-calculusgradientslinear-algebra

Builds on: Gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians, Four views of matrix multiplication · Leads to: Backpropagation via JVPs

Don't differentiate entry by entry. Nudge the input, keep only the part of the change that's linear in the nudge, and read the gradient off it.

The reading rule

The differential $df$ is the part of $f(x+dx)-f(x)$ that is linear in the nudge $dx$. Whatever multiplies $dx$ is the gradient.

From the gradient note, $f(x+dx)-f(x)\approx\nabla f^Tdx$. So if algebra hands you $df=g^Tdx$ for some vector $g$, then $g=\nabla f$:

$$df=\underbrace{\nabla f^T}_{1\times n}\underbrace{dx}_{n\times1} \ \Rightarrow\ \nabla f.$$

For a matrix input, the same first-order change is a sum over every entry (each $X_{ij}$ is just another coordinate):

$$df=\sum_{ij}\frac{\partial f}{\partial X_{ij}}\,dX_{ij}=\sum_{ij}G_{ij}\,dX_{ij}, \qquad G_{ij}:=\frac{\partial f}{\partial X_{ij}}.$$

Write that sum as a trace: diagonal entry $j$ of $G^TdX$ is (column $j$ of $G$) $\cdot$ (column $j$ of $dX$), so

$$\operatorname{tr}(G^TdX)=\sum_j\sum_iG_{ij}\,dX_{ij}.$$

$$df=\operatorname{tr}\big(G^T\,dX\big) \ \Rightarrow\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial X}=G.$$

$G_{ij}$ sits where $X_{ij}$ sits, so the gradient with respect to $X$ always has the same shape as $X$.

Toolkit

Four rules, each one line from the definitions. Together they turn any $df$ into the form $\operatorname{tr}(G^TdX)$.

Product rule$d(XY)=dX\,Y+X\,dY$
Constants pass through$d(AXB)=A\,dX\,B$
A scalar is its own trace$s=\operatorname{tr}(s)$
Trace is cyclic$\operatorname{tr}(ABC)=\operatorname{tr}(CAB)$

Product rule. Expand and drop the term that is second order in the nudges:

$$(X+dX)(Y+dY)-XY=dX\,Y+X\,dY+\underbrace{dX\,dY}_{\text{2nd order}}.$$

Constants. Apply the product rule twice with $dA=dB=0$. Scalar = trace: a $1\times1$ matrix is its own diagonal.

Cyclic trace. First for two factors, by swapping the order of summation:

$$\operatorname{tr}(PQ)=\sum_i\sum_jP_{ij}Q_{ji}=\sum_j\sum_iQ_{ji}P_{ij}=\operatorname{tr}(QP).$$

Then take $P=AB$, $Q=C$: $\operatorname{tr}(ABC)=\operatorname{tr}(CAB)$.

Example: $f=\tfrac12x^TAx$, $A$ symmetric

Product rule on the factors $x^T$ and $Ax$ ($A$ is constant):

$$df=\tfrac12\big(dx^T\,Ax+x^TA\,dx\big).$$

The first term is a scalar, so it equals its transpose: $dx^TAx=x^TA^Tdx=x^TA\,dx$ (as $A^T=A$). The two halves are equal:

$$df=x^TA\,dx=(Ax)^Tdx \ \Rightarrow\ \nabla f=Ax.$$

At $(1,1)$ with $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$: $Ax=(2+1,\ 1+2)=(3,3)$, matching Gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians. ✓

Example: a linear layer

The weight gradient is an outer product: error times input.

$L=\tfrac12\|Wx-y\|^2=\tfrac12r^Tr$ with $r=Wx-y$, $W$ of shape $k\times d$. Only $W$ moves, so $dr=dW\,x$. Then

$$dL=\tfrac12(dr^Tr+r^Tdr)=r^Tdr\qquad(\text{scalar}=\text{its transpose})$$

$$=r^T\,dW\,x=\operatorname{tr}(r^T\,dW\,x)\qquad(\text{scalar}=\text{trace})$$

$$=\operatorname{tr}(x\,r^T\,dW)\qquad(\text{cyclic: move }x\text{ to the front}).$$

Match with $\operatorname{tr}(G^TdW)$: $G^T=xr^T$, so

$$\underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial W}}_{k\times d}=G=\underbrace{r}_{k\times1}\underbrace{x^T}_{1\times d}.$$

Check one entry directly: $L=\tfrac12\sum_i\big(\sum_jW_{ij}x_j-y_i\big)^2$, and only term $i$ contains $W_{ij}$, so $\partial L/\partial W_{ij}=r_i\,x_j=(rx^T)_{ij}$. ✓

Two you'll meet in papers

Both come from a known identity plus the toolkit: differentiate $XX^{-1}=I$ for the inverse, and factor $X$ out of the determinant for log det.

Inverse. $XX^{-1}=I$ is constant, so its differential is zero. Product rule:

$$dX\,X^{-1}+X\,d(X^{-1})=0.$$

Multiply on the left by $X^{-1}$:

$$d(X^{-1})=-X^{-1}\,dX\,X^{-1}.$$

For $1\times1$ this is $d(1/x)=-dx/x^2$. ✓

Log determinant. Factor out $X$, using $\det(PQ)=\det P\det Q$:

$$\det(X+dX)=\det X\cdot\det(I+E), \qquad E:=X^{-1}dX.$$

If $E$ has eigenvalues $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_n$ (small, since $E$ is), then $I+E$ has eigenvalues $1+\mu_i$ (same eigenvectors). The determinant is the product of the eigenvalues and the trace is their sum:

$$\det(I+E)=\prod_i(1+\mu_i)=1+\sum_i\mu_i+\underbrace{\sum_{i\lt j}\mu_i\mu_j+\cdots}_{\text{2nd order}}$$

$$\approx1+\operatorname{tr}E.$$

Take logs of the first line, and use $\log(1+\epsilon)\approx\epsilon$:

$$d\log\det X=\log\det(I+E)\approx\operatorname{tr}E=\operatorname{tr}(X^{-1}dX).$$

Reading rule: $G^T=X^{-1}$, so $\partial\log\det X/\partial X=X^{-T}$. Check on $X=\operatorname{diag}(a,b)$: $\log\det X=\log a+\log b$, whose partials $1/a$, $1/b$ are the diagonal of $X^{-1}$. ✓

Derive it yourself

Least squares: $L(w)=\tfrac12\|Xw-y\|^2$. Find $\nabla L$ and set it to zero.

Solution

Same steps as the linear layer, but now $w$ moves and $X$ is fixed. With $r=Xw-y$, $dr=X\,dw$, and

$$dL=\tfrac12(dr^Tr+r^Tdr)=r^Tdr=r^TX\,dw=(X^Tr)^Tdw.$$

Reading rule: $\nabla L=X^T(Xw-y)$. Setting it to zero gives $X^TXw=X^Ty$: the normal equations from Rank, subspaces, and projections.
