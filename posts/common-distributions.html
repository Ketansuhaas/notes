Common distributions, and where they come from in deep learning

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran · 30 October 2026
probabilitydeep-learning

A quick reference, not a course: for each distribution, its pmf/pdf, mean and variance, and the one-line reason it is the default choice for a particular kind of output (from Probability notation and random variables).

Bernoulli($p$)

$$ p(x) = p^x(1-p)^{1-x}, \ x\in\{0,1\}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[X]=p, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=p(1-p). $$

Intuition: Bernoulli is the distribution of a single yes/no outcome with no other structure, which is exactly a binary label; a classifier's sigmoid output is interpreted as the $p$ of a Bernoulli over $y$.

Categorical($p_1,\dots,p_K$)

$$ p(x=k) = p_k, \ \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^K p_k=1, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{1}[X=k]]=p_k. $$

A softmax layer $p_k=\dfrac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}$ turns arbitrary real logits $z\in\mathbb{R}^K$ into a valid Categorical parameter vector.

Intuition: Categorical is Bernoulli generalized from 2 outcomes to $K$; it is the only distribution over a finite unordered set of labels, which is exactly a multi-class label, with softmax doing the job of mapping unconstrained network output onto the probability simplex.

Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$

$$ p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \qquad \mathbb{E}[X]=\mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(X)=\sigma^2. $$

Shows up as: regression targets ("predict a number"), weight initialization, the latent prior in a VAE, and the noise added/removed at each diffusion step.

Intuition: the Gaussian is the maximum-entropy distribution for a fixed mean and variance — the least additional assumption you can make beyond "it has this average and this spread." That is exactly the right amount of assumption when you have no reason to expect anything more specific, so "predict a number, assume Gaussian noise" is the default.

Multivariate Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)$

$$ p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^d\det\Sigma}}\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\right), \qquad \underbrace{\mu}_{d\times1}, \ \underbrace{\Sigma}_{d\times d}\text{ (symmetric, PSD)}. $$

$(x-\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)$ is exactly the quadratic form from Quadratic forms and positive definiteness, and $\Sigma$'s eigenvectors/eigenvalues are its principal axes/variances, as in Principal component analysis. In practice $\Sigma$ is usually restricted to diagonal ($d$ numbers instead of $\tfrac{d(d+1)}2$), i.e. independent coordinates with their own variances, since the full covariance is expensive to parameterize and invert in high dimensions.

Intuition: the multivariate Gaussian is $d$ single Gaussians coupled by a covariance matrix; dropping the off-diagonal terms is exactly the independence assumption $p(x)=\prod_i p(x_i)$ from the notation post, traded for tractability.

A couple more, briefly

Laplace($\mu,b$): $p(x)=\tfrac1{2b}\exp(-|x-\mu|/b)$, a sharper peak and heavier tails than Gaussian. Maximizing its log-likelihood is exactly minimizing $|x-\mu|$, so a Laplace noise assumption is what sits behind an L1 (mean absolute error) loss, the way Gaussian sits behind L2 (MSE).

Uniform($a,b$): $p(x)=\tfrac1{b-a}$ on $[a,b]$, 0 elsewhere — used for weight initialization ranges and for sampling augmentation parameters, whenever every value in a range is meant to be equally likely a priori.

Intuition: the common thread across this whole post — every distribution here is a noise/shape assumption baked into a loss function or a module. Supervised learning as KL minimization makes that connection exact: choosing a distributional assumption for $p_\theta(y\mid x)$ and maximizing likelihood is where MSE and cross-entropy actually come from.
