Common distributions, and where they come from in deep learning

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran
probabilitydeep-learningstatisticsdistributionsgaussian

Builds on: Probability notation, Expectation and variance algebra, The covariance matrix, intuitively · Leads to: Supervised learning as KL minimization, The reparameterization trick

Each distribution is an assumption about one kind of output. Choose the output's distribution and $-\log p$ hands you the loss.

Bernoulli($p$)

One yes/no outcome: a binary label. A sigmoid output is its $p$.

$$p(x)=p^x(1-p)^{1-x},\ x\in\{0,1\}, \qquad \mathbb{E}X=p, \qquad \operatorname{Var}X=p(1-p).$$

The exponents just select: $x=1$ gives $p^1(1-p)^0=p$, $x=0$ gives $1-p$. Moments, using $X^2=X$ for $X\in\{0,1\}$ and $\operatorname{Var}X=\mathbb{E}X^2-(\mathbb{E}X)^2$ (variance algebra):

$$\mathbb{E}X=0\cdot(1-p)+1\cdot p=p, \qquad \operatorname{Var}X=\mathbb{E}X-(\mathbb{E}X)^2=p-p^2=p(1-p).$$

Take $-\log$ of the pmf at the label $y$: the exponents come down as weights,

$$-\log p(y)=-\big[y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p)\big] \quad(\text{binary cross-entropy}).$$

Example

Model says $p=0.9$. Label $y=1$: $-\log0.9\approx0.11$. Label $y=0$: $-\log(1-0.9)=-\log0.1\approx2.30$.

Categorical($p_1,\dots,p_K$)

Bernoulli with $K$ outcomes. Softmax turns any real logits into valid probabilities.

$$p(x=k)=p_k,\ \ \textstyle\sum_kp_k=1, \qquad \underbrace{p}_{K\times1}=\operatorname{softmax}(\underbrace{z}_{K\times1}),\ \ p_k=\dfrac{e^{z_k}}{\sum_je^{z_j}}.$$

Example

Why softmax is valid: $e^{z_k}\gt0$ makes every $p_k\gt0$, and dividing by the total makes them sum to $1$:

$$\sum_kp_k=\frac{\sum_ke^{z_k}}{\sum_je^{z_j}}=1.$$

With label $y$, $-\log p(x=y)=-\log p_y$: cross-entropy.

Example

$z=(2,1,0)$: $e^z\approx(7.389,\,2.718,\,1)$, sum $11.107$, so

$$p\approx\Big(\tfrac{7.389}{11.107},\ \tfrac{2.718}{11.107},\ \tfrac{1}{11.107}\Big)\approx(0.67,\,0.24,\,0.09).$$

Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)$

The least-committal choice given only a mean and a spread (maximum entropy). Regression noise, initialization, VAE priors, diffusion noise.

$$p(x)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big), \qquad \mathbb{E}X=\mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}X=\sigma^2.$$

Step 1: the constant, $\int e^{-x^2/2}dx=\sqrt{2\pi}$. Call the integral $I$. Square it, read the product as a 2D integral, and switch to polar coordinates ($x^2+y^2=r^2$, $dx\,dy=r\,dr\,d\theta$):

$$I^2=\int\!\!\int e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}\,dx\,dy=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta.$$

The extra $r$ is what makes it solvable: $\tfrac{d}{dr}\big(-e^{-r^2/2}\big)=r\,e^{-r^2/2}$. So

$$I^2=2\pi\Big[-e^{-r^2/2}\Big]_0^\infty=2\pi(0+1)=2\pi \ \Rightarrow\ I=\sqrt{2\pi}.$$

Step 2: shift and scale. Substitute $x=\mu+\sigma t$, $dx=\sigma\,dt$:

$$\int e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)}\,dx=\sigma\int e^{-t^2/2}\,dt=\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}=\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}.$$

Dividing by this makes the area $1$. It also says $X=\mu+\sigma T$ with $T\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$.

Step 3: moments of $T$. $t\,e^{-t^2/2}$ is odd, so $\mathbb{E}T=0$. For $\mathbb{E}T^2$, integrate by parts with $u=t$, $dv=t\,e^{-t^2/2}dt$, $v=-e^{-t^2/2}$:

$$\int t^2e^{-t^2/2}\,dt=\Big[-t\,e^{-t^2/2}\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+\int e^{-t^2/2}\,dt=0+\sqrt{2\pi},$$

so $\mathbb{E}T^2=\sqrt{2\pi}/\sqrt{2\pi}=1$. Then shift and scale (variance algebra): $\mathbb{E}X=\mu+\sigma\cdot0=\mu$, $\operatorname{Var}X=\sigma^2\operatorname{Var}T=\sigma^2$.

Proof idea: why maximum entropy. Maximize $-\int p\log p$ subject to $\int p=1$, $\int xp=\mu$, $\int(x-\mu)^2p=\sigma^2$. Setting the Lagrangian's derivative in each value $p(x)$ to zero:

$$-\log p(x)-1+a+b\,x+c\,(x-\mu)^2=0 \ \Rightarrow\ p(x)=e^{\text{quadratic in }x},$$

a Gaussian once the constraints fix $a,b,c$. A full proof shows this stationary point is the maximum (entropy is concave, or use $\mathrm{KL}\ge0$).

Example

Predict $\mu=f(x)$ with fixed $\sigma$. Take $-\log$ of the density at the target $y$:

$$-\log p(y)=\frac{(y-f(x))^2}{2\sigma^2}+\underbrace{\tfrac12\log(2\pi\sigma^2)}_{\text{const}}.$$

Minimizing it over $f$ is minimizing squared error. That is MSE.

Multivariate Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)$

Contours are ellipses with the covariance cloud's axes. Diagonal $\Sigma$ means independent coordinates.

$$ p(x)=\frac1{\sqrt{(2\pi)^d\det\Sigma}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)\Big), \qquad \underbrace{\mu}_{d\times1},\ \underbrace{\Sigma}_{d\times d}\succ0. $$

($\Sigma\succ0$, positive definite, so that $\Sigma^{-1}$ exists and $\det\Sigma\gt0$; a singular $\Sigma$ puts all mass on a flat subspace and has no density.)

The constant: rotate to the axes. Spectral theorem: $\Sigma=Q\Lambda Q^T$, so $\Sigma^{-1}=Q\Lambda^{-1}Q^T$. Set $z=Q^T(x-\mu)$:

$$(x-\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)=z^T\Lambda^{-1}z=\sum_i\frac{z_i^2}{\lambda_i}.$$

A rotation keeps volume ($|\det Q|=1$, so $dx=dz$), and the exponential of a sum is a product of 1D Gaussian integrals (Step 2 above, with $\sigma^2=\lambda_i$):

$$\int e^{-\frac12\sum_iz_i^2/\lambda_i}\,dz=\prod_i\sqrt{2\pi\lambda_i}=\sqrt{(2\pi)^d\textstyle\prod_i\lambda_i}=\sqrt{(2\pi)^d\det\Sigma}.$$

So in the axes the coordinates $z_i$ are independent $\mathcal N(0,\lambda_i)$, and $\operatorname{Cov}(x)=Q\Lambda Q^T=\Sigma$ by the $A\Sigma A^T$ rule.

$$\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^2,\dots,\sigma_d^2) \iff p(x)=\textstyle\prod_ip(x_i) \qquad (d \text{ parameters instead of } \tfrac{d(d+1)}2).$$

$\Rightarrow$: with diagonal $\Sigma$ the quadratic form is $\sum_i(x_i-\mu_i)^2/\sigma_i^2$ and $\det\Sigma=\prod_i\sigma_i^2$, so the density splits into a product of 1D Gaussians. $\Leftarrow$: independent coordinates have $\operatorname{Cov}(x_i,x_j)=0$ (variance algebra). Count: a symmetric $\Sigma$ has $d$ diagonal entries plus $\tfrac{d(d-1)}2$ above it, $\tfrac{d(d+1)}2$ in all.

Example

$\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, $\mu=0$: eigenvalues $3,1$ along $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$ (covariance note). In eigen-coordinates the contour $x^T\Sigma^{-1}x=1$ reads $\tfrac{z_1^2}3+\tfrac{z_2^2}1=1$: semi-axes $\sqrt3$ along $(1,1)$ and $1$ along $(1,-1)$.

Check one end: $x=\sqrt3\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, with $\Sigma^{-1}=\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$:

$$x^T\Sigma^{-1}x=\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac13\,(2-1-1+2)=\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac23=1. \ \checkmark$$

Laplace: each side of $\mu$ has area $\int_0^\infty e^{-t/b}dt=b$, total $2b$, hence the $\tfrac1{2b}$. Then

$$-\log p(y)=\frac{|y-\mu|}b+\log2b \quad(\text{absolute error, L1}).$$

Uniform: height $\tfrac1{b-a}$ times width $b-a$ is area $1$.

Derive it yourself

Show softmax ignores a shift of all logits, $z\to z+c\mathbf{1}$, and that for $K=2$ it is a sigmoid: $p_1=\sigma(z_1-z_2)$ with $\sigma(t)=\frac1{1+e^{-t}}$.

Solution

$\dfrac{e^{z_k+c}}{\sum_je^{z_j+c}}=\dfrac{e^ce^{z_k}}{e^c\sum_je^{z_j}}$: the $e^c$ cancels. $K=2$: divide top and bottom by $e^{z_1}$, $p_1=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{z_2-z_1}}=\sigma(z_1-z_2)$. So Categorical with $K=2$ is Bernoulli.
