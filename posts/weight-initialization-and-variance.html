Weight initialization and variance propagation

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran

Builds on: Expectation and variance algebra, Backpropagation via JVPs, Dynamics in the eigenbasis · Leads to: normalization layers (coming)

Every layer multiplies the signal's variance by the same factor. Depth raises that factor to the power $L$, so initialization sets it to $1$.

One layer: factor $n_{\text{in}}\sigma_W^2$

Each output sums $n_{\text{in}}$ zero-mean, uncorrelated terms, so their variances add.

Setup: $\underbrace{y}_{n_{\text{out}}\times1}=\underbrace{W}_{n_{\text{out}}\times n_{\text{in}}}\underbrace{x}_{n_{\text{in}}\times1}$, so $y_j=\sum_iW_{ji}x_i$. Assume $W_{ji}$ iid with mean $0$, variance $\sigma_W^2$, independent of $x$, and the $x_i$ identically distributed.

Mean. Independence lets the expectation of a product factor:

$$\mathbb{E}[y_j]=\sum_i\mathbb{E}[W_{ji}]\,\mathbb{E}[x_i]=\sum_i0\cdot\mathbb{E}[x_i]=0.$$

Variance. Mean zero, so $\operatorname{Var}(y_j)=\mathbb{E}[y_j^2]$. Expand the square as a double sum, then factor by independence of $W$ and $x$:

$$\mathbb{E}[y_j^2]=\sum_{i,k}\mathbb{E}[W_{ji}W_{jk}\,x_ix_k]=\sum_{i,k}\mathbb{E}[W_{ji}W_{jk}]\,\mathbb{E}[x_ix_k].$$

For $i\ne k$ the weights are independent with mean $0$: $\mathbb{E}[W_{ji}W_{jk}]=0\cdot0$. For $i=k$ it is $\mathbb{E}[W_{ji}^2]=\sigma_W^2$. Only the diagonal survives:

$$\operatorname{Var}(y_j)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\text{in}}}\sigma_W^2\,\mathbb{E}[x_i^2]=n_{\text{in}}\,\sigma_W^2\,\mathbb{E}[x^2].$$

The cross terms die because of $W$, so the $x_i$ may even be correlated. For zero-mean $x$: $\mathbb{E}[x^2]=\operatorname{Var}(x)$.

Depth: the factor to the power $L$

As in Dynamics in the eigenbasis: repeated multiplication explodes above $1$ and dies below $1$.

Take $L$ linear layers $h_\ell=W_\ell h_{\ell-1}$, all of width $n_{\text{in}}$, with $h_0=x$ zero-mean. $W_\ell$ is drawn independently of the earlier weights, so it is independent of its input $h_{\ell-1}$, and the one-layer result applies at every layer. Each $h_\ell$ is zero-mean (shown above), so $\mathbb{E}[h_\ell^2]=\operatorname{Var}(h_\ell)$ and

$$\operatorname{Var}(h_\ell)=n_{\text{in}}\sigma_W^2\operatorname{Var}(h_{\ell-1})$$ $$\Rightarrow\ \operatorname{Var}(h_L)=(n_{\text{in}}\sigma_W^2)^L\operatorname{Var}(x)\quad\text{(apply } L \text{ times)}.$$

The only factor that neither explodes nor vanishes for every $L$ is $1$:

$$n_{\text{in}}\sigma_W^2=1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{LeCun: } \sigma_W^2=\frac1{n_{\text{in}}}.$$

Example: $n_{\text{in}}=512$, $10$ linear layers

$\sigma_W=1$factor $512\cdot1^2=512$$512^{10}=2^{90}\approx1.2\times10^{27}$: explodes
$\sigma_W=0.01$factor $512\cdot10^{-4}=0.0512$$0.0512^{10}=10^{10\log_{10}0.0512}=10^{-12.9}\approx1.2\times10^{-13}$: vanishes

Backward pass: Xavier

Gradients flow back through $W$ from the other side, summing over $n_{\text{out}}$ terms. One $\sigma_W^2$ can't fix both directions unless $n_{\text{in}}=n_{\text{out}}$, so average.

The backward recursion for $y=Wx$ is a row times $W$, so each input-gradient entry sums over the $n_{\text{out}}$ outputs (column $i$ of $W$):

$$\underbrace{g_x}_{1\times n_{\text{in}}}=\underbrace{g_y}_{1\times n_{\text{out}}}\underbrace{W}_{n_{\text{out}}\times n_{\text{in}}} \ \Rightarrow\ g_{x,i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\text{out}}}g_{y,j}\,W_{ji}.$$

This is the one-layer sum again, with $n_{\text{out}}$ terms instead of $n_{\text{in}}$. Assume $g_y$ is independent of $W$ (only approximately true, since $g_y$ depends on the forward pass through $W$; this is Glorot's standard assumption). Then the same double-sum argument, with $g_{y,j}$ in place of $x_i$, gives mean $0$ and

$$\operatorname{Var}(g_{x,i})=n_{\text{out}}\,\sigma_W^2\,\mathbb{E}[g_y^2].$$

Setting each factor to $1$ gives two demands. One $\sigma_W^2$ can't meet both unless $n_{\text{in}}=n_{\text{out}}$, so ask instead that the average of the two factors be $1$:

$$\tfrac12\big(n_{\text{in}}\sigma_W^2+n_{\text{out}}\sigma_W^2\big)=1 \ \Rightarrow\ \sigma_W^2=\frac{2}{n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}}}.$$

Equivalently, $2/(n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}})$ is the harmonic mean of $1/n_{\text{in}}$ and $1/n_{\text{out}}$, so it always lies between the two demands.

Example

$256\to1024$: LeCun $1/256$, Xavier $2/(256+1024)=2/1280=1/640$. The two factors under Xavier: forward $256/640=0.4$, backward $1024/640=1.6$, average $(0.4+1.6)/2=1$. ✓

ReLU halves the second moment: He

ReLU zeros the negative half. For a symmetric pre-activation, the kept half carries exactly half of $\mathbb{E}[z^2]$, so double $\sigma_W^2$.

Why $z$ is symmetric. $z=Wx$ with $W$ zero-mean and symmetric (Gaussian or uniform), independent of $x$. Then $-W$ has the same distribution as $W$, so $-z=(-W)x$ has the same distribution as $z$.

Halving, by symmetry. $\mathrm{ReLU}(z)^2=z^2\,\mathbf 1_{z\gt0}$. Swap $z\to-z$ (same distribution):

$$\mathbb{E}[z^2\mathbf 1_{z\gt0}]=\mathbb{E}[(-z)^2\mathbf 1_{-z\gt0}]=\mathbb{E}[z^2\mathbf 1_{z\lt0}].$$

The two halves add to the whole ($z=0$ contributes $z^2=0$), and they are equal, so each is half:

$$\mathbb{E}[z^2\mathbf 1_{z\gt0}]+\mathbb{E}[z^2\mathbf 1_{z\lt0}]=\mathbb{E}[z^2] \ \Rightarrow\ \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{ReLU}(z)^2]=\tfrac12\mathbb{E}[z^2].$$

One layer. Feed $x=\mathrm{ReLU}(z_\ell)$ into the one-layer rule, then use $\mathbb{E}[z_\ell^2]=\operatorname{Var}(z_\ell)$ ($z_\ell$ is zero-mean):

$$\operatorname{Var}(z_{\ell+1})=n_{\text{in}}\sigma_W^2\,\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{ReLU}(z_\ell)^2]=n_{\text{in}}\sigma_W^2\cdot\tfrac12\operatorname{Var}(z_\ell).$$

Set the factor $\tfrac12n_{\text{in}}\sigma_W^2=1$:

$$\text{He: } \sigma_W^2=\frac2{n_{\text{in}}}.$$

ReLU outputs aren't zero-mean, which is why the layer rule was written with $\mathbb{E}[x^2]$: only the second moment enters (the cross terms died because of $W$, not $x$).

Example

$n_{\text{in}}=512$: He $\sigma_W=\sqrt{2/512}=\sqrt{1/256}=1/16=0.0625$; LeCun $\sigma_W=\sqrt{1/512}\approx0.044$. The ratio is $\sqrt2$, from the factor $\tfrac12$.

Derive it yourself

Frameworks often sample $W_{ji}\sim\mathcal U(-a,a)$. Find the $a$ that gives the Xavier variance $2/(n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}})$.

Solution

The density of $\mathcal U(-a,a)$ is $\frac1{2a}$ on $[-a,a]$. The mean is $0$ (symmetric about $0$), so the variance is the second moment:

$$\operatorname{Var}(W_{ji})=\mathbb{E}[W_{ji}^2]=\int_{-a}^a\frac{w^2}{2a}\,dw=\frac1{2a}\Big[\frac{w^3}3\Big]_{-a}^{a}=\frac1{2a}\cdot\frac{2a^3}3=\frac{a^2}3.$$

Match it to Xavier and solve for $a$:

$$\frac{a^2}3=\frac2{n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}}} \ \Rightarrow\ a^2=\frac6{n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}}} \ \Rightarrow\ a=\sqrt{\frac6{n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}}}},$$

the formula in the Glorot paper. He-uniform likewise: $\frac{a^2}3=\frac2{n_{\text{in}}}\Rightarrow a=\sqrt{6/n_{\text{in}}}$.
