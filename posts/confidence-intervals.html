Confidence intervals: the z and t statistics

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran

Builds on: LLN and CLT, Sample variance and degrees of freedom · Leads to: Entropy and cross-entropy

What "95%" claims

$\mu$ is fixed; the interval is random. "95%" describes the procedure: repeat it many times and 95% of the intervals catch $\mu$. Any one interval either hit or missed.

$$P\big(L(x_{1:n})\le\mu\le U(x_{1:n})\big)=1-\alpha.$$

The probability is over samples (random $L,U$), not over $\mu$.

$\sigma$ known: the z-interval

Estimate $\pm$ margin. The margin is the standard error $\sigma/\sqrt n$ times how many standard deviations the normal needs to hold $1-\alpha$ of its mass.

Step 1: standardize. From the sample-variance note, $\mathbb{E}[\bar x_n]=\mu$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\bar x_n)=\sigma^2/n$. Subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation (the standard error $\sigma/\sqrt n$):

$$Z_n=\frac{\bar x_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}: \qquad \mathbb{E}[Z_n]=0, \quad \operatorname{Var}(Z_n)=\frac{\sigma^2/n}{\sigma^2/n}=1.$$

By the CLT $Z_n\approx\mathcal N(0,1)$ (exactly, if the $x_i$ are Gaussian).

Step 2: the critical value. $z_{\alpha/2}$ is the point with $\alpha/2$ of the normal's mass to its right. It comes from a normal table (or norm.ppf(0.975)), not from algebra: $\Phi(1.96)\approx0.975$. By symmetry,

$$P(|Z|\le1.96)=\Phi(1.96)-\Phi(-1.96)=0.975-0.025=0.95.$$

Step 3: rearrange the event so $\mu$ stands alone. Multiply through by $\sigma/\sqrt n\gt0$, subtract $\bar x_n$, multiply by $-1$ (flips the inequalities):

$$-1.96\le\frac{\bar x_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\le1.96 \iff -1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le\bar x_n-\mu\le1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}$$

$$\iff \bar x_n-1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le\mu\le\bar x_n+1.96\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.$$

Same event, so same probability $0.95$. In general:

$$\boxed{\ \mu\in\bar x_n\pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\ }$$

Example

$n=100$, $\bar x=5.2$, $\sigma=2$:

$$\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}=\frac2{10}=0.2, \qquad 1.96\cdot0.2=0.392, \qquad [5.2-0.392,\ 5.2+0.392]=[4.81,\ 5.59].$$

$\sigma$ unknown: the t-interval

Plug in $\hat\sigma$ (divide by $n-1$). But $\hat\sigma$ is random too: when it comes out small, $T_n$ overshoots. So $T_n$ needs heavier tails than $\mathcal N(0,1)$.

Step 1: split $T_n$ into known pieces. Multiply top and bottom by $\sigma$:

$$T_n=\frac{\bar x_n-\mu}{\hat\sigma/\sqrt n}=\frac{(\bar x_n-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)}{\hat\sigma/\sigma}=\frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/(n-1)}},$$

$$\text{where}\quad V:=\frac{(n-1)\hat\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}, \quad\text{so}\quad \frac{V}{n-1}=\frac{\hat\sigma^2}{\sigma^2}.$$

Step 2: what each piece is (Gaussian data). From the rotation argument in the sample-variance note:

$$Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1), \qquad V=\sum_{k=2}^n\Big(\frac{z_k}\sigma\Big)^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}, \qquad Z \text{ independent of } V.$$

A normal divided by $\sqrt{\chi^2_k/k}$, independent, is by definition Student's $t_k$. So

$$T_n\sim t_{n-1} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mu\in\bar x_n\pm t_{n-1,\alpha/2}\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt n} \quad\text{(rearrange exactly as in the z case)}.$$

Proof idea (why the tails are heavier, and why $t\to\mathcal N$). $\mathbb{E}[V/(n-1)]=\mathbb{E}[\hat\sigma^2]/\sigma^2=1$, but it fluctuates: each $(z_k/\sigma)^2$ has variance $\mathbb{E}[Z^4]-1=3-1=2$, so

$$\operatorname{Var}\Big(\frac V{n-1}\Big)=\frac{(n-1)\cdot2}{(n-1)^2}=\frac2{n-1}.$$

When $V/(n-1)$ lands below $1$, it inflates $T_n$: extra tail mass. As $n\to\infty$ that variance $\to0$, so $V/(n-1)\to1$ and $T_n\to Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. A full proof writes the joint density of $(Z,V)$ and changes variables to get the $t$ density $\propto(1+t^2/k)^{-(k+1)/2}$. Exact for Gaussian $x_i$; for other data it is an approximation (CLT for the top, LLN for the bottom).

The critical values are table lookups (or t.ppf(0.975, n-1)), rounded:

$n$$5$$10$$30$$\infty$
$t_{n-1,\,0.025}$$2.78$$2.26$$2.05$$1.96$

Example

$n=10$, $\bar x=5.2$, $\hat\sigma=2$:

$$\frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt n}=\frac2{3.162}=0.632, \qquad \text{t-margin } 2.26\cdot0.632=1.43, \qquad \text{z-margin } 1.96\cdot0.632=1.24.$$

$1.43/1.24\approx1.15$: not knowing $\sigma$ costs a 15% wider interval at $n=10$.

Side by side

$\sigma$ known$\bar x_n\pm z_{\alpha/2}\,\sigma/\sqrt n$
$\sigma$ estimated$\bar x_n\pm t_{n-1,\alpha/2}\,\hat\sigma/\sqrt n$

Derive it yourself

Solve $-z\le Z_n\le z$ for $\mu$ to get the z-interval. Then find how large $n$ must be for a 95% half-width of at most $E$. Try $\sigma=2$, $E=0.1$.

Solution

Multiply by $\sigma/\sqrt n\gt0$, then subtract $\bar x_n$ and flip signs:

$$-z\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le\bar x_n-\mu\le z\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \iff \bar x_n-z\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le\mu\le\bar x_n+z\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.$$

Half-width at most $E$; solve for $n$ (both sides positive, so squaring keeps the order):

$$z\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\le E \iff \sqrt n\ge\frac{z\sigma}E \iff n\ge\Big(\frac{z\sigma}E\Big)^2.$$

$$\sigma=2,\ E=0.1:\quad \frac{1.96\cdot2}{0.1}=39.2, \qquad 39.2^2=1536.64 \ \Rightarrow\ n=1537 \text{ (round up)}.$$

Since $n\propto1/E^2$, halving the half-width costs $4\times$ the data.
