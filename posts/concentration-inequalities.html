Concentration inequalities

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran
probabilitystatisticsconcentration-inequalities

Builds on: Expectation and variance algebra, Common distributions · Leads to: Law of large numbers and the central limit theorem, Confidence intervals

How likely is a random quantity to land far from its mean? Every bound here is one trick, Markov, applied to a smarter non-negative function.

Markov

A non-negative variable can't put much mass far out without dragging its mean up. Needs only $X\ge0$ and a mean.

$$X\ge0,\ a\gt0: \qquad P(X\ge a)\le\frac{\mathbb{E}X}{a}.$$

Proof. Compare two variables at every outcome. If $X\ge a$: left side $a\le X$. If $X\lt a$: left side $0\le X$ (since $X\ge0$). So pointwise

$$a\,\mathbb{1}[X\ge a]\le X \ \overset{\mathbb{E}}{\Longrightarrow}\ a\,\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\,\mathbb{1}[X\ge a]}_{P(X\ge a)}\le\mathbb{E}X,$$

and divide by $a$. (Taking $\mathbb{E}$ keeps $\le$: it is a weighted average with non-negative weights.)

Example

Mean wait $2$ min: $P(\text{wait}\ge10)\le\tfrac2{10}=0.2$, whatever the distribution.

Chebyshev

Apply Markov to the squared distance from the mean. Knowing the spread bounds both tails.

$Y=(X-\mu)^2$ is non-negative with $\mathbb{E}Y=\sigma^2$. Squaring is increasing on non-negatives, so $|X-\mu|\ge k$ is the same event as $Y\ge k^2$. Markov on $Y$ with $a=k^2$:

$$P(|X-\mu|\ge k)=P\big((X-\mu)^2\ge k^2\big)\le\frac{\mathbb{E}Y}{k^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{k^2}.$$

Sample mean, $\operatorname{Var}\bar x_n=\sigma^2/n$ (variance algebra): substitute $\sigma^2\to\sigma^2/n$,

$$P(|\bar x_n-\mu|\ge k)\le\dfrac{\sigma^2}{nk^2}\to0.$$

That is the law of large numbers.

Example

$k=2\sigma$: $\tfrac{\sigma^2}{4\sigma^2}=\tfrac14$. For a Gaussian the truth is $P(|X-\mu|\ge2\sigma)=2(1-\Phi(2))\approx2\cdot0.0228=0.046$ ($\Phi$ the standard normal CDF, from a table), so the bound is loose but universal.

Chernoff

Apply Markov to $e^{tX}$. The exponential magnifies the tail, so the bound decays exponentially.

For $t\gt0$, $x\mapsto e^{tx}$ is increasing, so $X\ge a$ is the same event as $e^{tX}\ge e^{ta}$. Markov on the non-negative $e^{tX}$:

$$P(X\ge a)=P\big(e^{tX}\ge e^{ta}\big)\le e^{-ta}\,\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]}_{M_X(t)}.$$

This holds for every $t\gt0$, so keep the best one: $P(X\ge a)\le\min_{t\gt0}e^{-ta}M_X(t)$.

Example: $X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$

The MGF. Complete the square in the exponent, $tx-\tfrac{x^2}2=-\tfrac{(x-t)^2}2+\tfrac{t^2}2$; the shifted bell still integrates to $\sqrt{2\pi}$ (distributions note):

$$M_X(t)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{tx-x^2/2}\,dx=e^{t^2/2}\cdot\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-(x-t)^2/2}\,dx=e^{t^2/2}.$$

Optimize $t$. Minimize the exponent $g(t)=\tfrac{t^2}2-ta$: $g'(t)=t-a=0$ at $t=a$, where $g(a)=\tfrac{a^2}2-a^2=-\tfrac{a^2}2$. So

$$P(X\ge a)\le e^{-a^2/2}.$$

At $a=2$: $e^{-2}\approx0.135$, against Chebyshev's $0.25$ (valid here since $P(X\ge2)\le P(|X|\ge2)$). Further out the gap explodes: at $a=4$, $e^{-8}\approx3\times10^{-4}$ versus $\tfrac1{16}\approx0.06$.

Hoeffding

Bounded, independent samples: the sample mean's error probability shrinks exponentially in $n$.

$$x_i\in[a_i,b_i] \text{ indep.}: \quad P(|\bar x_n-\mu|\ge t)\le2\exp\!\Big(-\frac{2n^2t^2}{\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2}\Big)$$ $$\overset{a_i=a,\,b_i=b}{\Longrightarrow}\ P(|\bar x_n-\mu|\ge t)\le2\exp\!\Big(-\frac{2nt^2}{(b-a)^2}\Big).$$

Here $\mu=\tfrac1n\sum_i\mathbb{E}x_i$. It is Chernoff applied to the sum, with one ingredient taken as given.

Hoeffding's lemma (Proof idea). If $Y\in[a,b]$ and $\mathbb{E}Y=0$, then

$$\mathbb{E}[e^{sY}]\le e^{s^2(b-a)^2/8}.$$

Why: $e^{sy}$ is convex, so on $[a,b]$ it lies below its chord; taking $\mathbb{E}$ bounds $\mathbb{E}e^{sY}$ by the chord's value, a function of $s$ alone. Its log starts at $0$ with slope $0$ (mean zero) and second derivative a variance of a $[a,b]$-valued variable, at most $\tfrac{(b-a)^2}4$; Taylor gives $\le\tfrac{s^2}2\cdot\tfrac{(b-a)^2}4$. A full proof writes out the chord function and checks that bound.

Step 1: Chernoff on the sum. Let $Y_i=x_i-\mathbb{E}x_i\in[a_i-\mathbb{E}x_i,\ b_i-\mathbb{E}x_i]$, an interval of the same width $b_i-a_i$. The event $\bar x_n-\mu\ge t$ is $\sum_iY_i\ge nt$. For any $s\gt0$:

$$P\Big(\sum_iY_i\ge nt\Big)\le e^{-snt}\,\mathbb{E}\big[e^{s\sum_iY_i}\big]=e^{-snt}\prod_i\mathbb{E}\big[e^{sY_i}\big],$$

since $e^{s\sum Y_i}=\prod_ie^{sY_i}$ and $\mathbb{E}$ of a product of independent factors is the product of $\mathbb{E}$'s.

Step 2: the lemma on each factor. With $D=\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2$:

$$P\Big(\sum_iY_i\ge nt\Big)\le\exp\!\Big(-snt+\frac{s^2D}8\Big).$$

Step 3: optimize $s$. The exponent's derivative $-nt+\tfrac{sD}4=0$ gives $s=\tfrac{4nt}D$, and the exponent becomes

$$-\frac{4n^2t^2}D+\frac{16n^2t^2}{8D}=-\frac{2n^2t^2}D.$$

Step 4: both tails. $-Y_i$ satisfies the same conditions, so $P(\bar x_n-\mu\le-t)$ has the same bound; adding the two tails gives the factor $2$. Equal ranges: $D=n(b-a)^2$, so $\tfrac{2n^2t^2}D=\tfrac{2nt^2}{(b-a)^2}$.

Example

Coin flips in $[0,1]$, $t=0.1$, $n=1000$. Hoeffding: $\tfrac{2nt^2}{(b-a)^2}=2\cdot1000\cdot0.01=20$, so the bound is $2e^{-20}\approx4\times10^{-9}$.

Chebyshev needs $\sigma^2$. Any $X\in[0,1]$ has $X^2\le X$, so $\sigma^2=\mathbb{E}X^2-\mu^2\le\mu-\mu^2=\mu(1-\mu)\le\tfrac14$. Then $\tfrac{\sigma^2}{nt^2}\le\tfrac{0.25}{1000\cdot0.01}=0.025$.

The pattern

Markov$X\ge0$, mean$\sim1/a$
Chebyshev+ variance$\sim1/k^2$
Chernoff+ whole MGFexponential
Hoeffdingbounded, independent$\sim e^{-cnt^2}$

Each extra assumption buys a sharper tail. Confidence intervals and generalization bounds quote this shape.

Derive it yourself

From Hoeffding, how many samples $n$ guarantee $P(|\bar x_n-\mu|\ge t)\le\delta$ for $x_i\in[0,1]$? Evaluate at $t=0.1$, $\delta=0.05$.

Solution

Hoeffding with $b-a=1$. Set the bound $\le\delta$, take logs ($\log$ is increasing), and solve for $n$:

$$2e^{-2nt^2}\le\delta \iff -2nt^2\le\log\tfrac\delta2 \iff 2nt^2\ge\log\tfrac2\delta \iff n\ge\dfrac{\log(2/\delta)}{2t^2}.$$

Here $\tfrac2\delta=\tfrac2{0.05}=40$ and $2t^2=0.02$: $\dfrac{\log40}{0.02}\approx\dfrac{3.689}{0.02}\approx184.4$, so $n=185$. Note $n\propto1/t^2$: halving the error costs $4\times$ the data.
