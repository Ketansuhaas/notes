A float is scientific notation in binary. 16-bit floats halve the bytes and unlock tensor cores; the art is choosing which bits to give up.

Sign, exponent, mantissa

The exponent is the "$\times2^e$" part: how big or tiny a number can be (range). The mantissa is the "$1.xxx$" part: how many digits it keeps (precision).

$$x=(-1)^s\times1.m\times2^{\,e-\text{bias}}.$$

Here $s$ is the sign bit, $e$ is the exponent field read as an unsigned integer ($k$ bits), and $m$ is the $p$ mantissa bits written after a binary point. The leading $1.$ is not stored (every nonzero binary number starts with a 1, so it is free). Every row of the table below follows from this formula and the three bit counts.

Step 1: the bias. All-zeros $e$ is reserved for zero and subnormals, all-ones for $\infty$/NaN, so normal numbers use $e=1,\dots,2^k-2$. The bias $2^{k-1}-1$ centres that range on $0$:

$$\text{bias}=2^{k-1}-1:\qquad k=8\Rightarrow127,\qquad k=5\Rightarrow15.$$

$$e-\text{bias}\ \in\ \big[\,1-\text{bias},\ (2^k-2)-\text{bias}\,\big]=\big[\,1-\text{bias},\ \text{bias}\,\big].$$

$$\text{fp32, bf16: }[-126,\,127],\qquad \text{fp16: }[-14,\,15].$$

Step 2: largest. Top exponent $\text{bias}$, all mantissa bits 1, so $1.m=1.11\ldots1_2=2-2^{-p}$:

$$x_{\max}=(2-2^{-p})\cdot2^{\text{bias}}.$$

$$\text{fp32: }(2-2^{-23})\,2^{127}\approx3.40\times10^{38},\qquad \text{bf16: }(2-2^{-7})\,2^{127}\approx3.39\times10^{38}.$$

$$\text{fp16: }(2-2^{-10})\,2^{15}=2^{16}-2^{5}=65536-32=65504.$$

Step 3: spacing. Between $2^E$ and $2^{E+1}$ the exponent is fixed and the last mantissa bit is worth $2^{-p}$, so neighbours differ by

$$\text{gap}(x)=2^{E}\cdot2^{-p}\quad\text{for }2^E\le x\lt2^{E+1}.$$

At $x=1$ ($E=0$) this is machine epsilon $\varepsilon=2^{-p}$: fp32 $2^{-23}$, bf16 $2^{-7}$, fp16 $2^{-10}$. Rounding to nearest is off by at most half a gap, a relative error of at most $\varepsilon/2$.

Step 4: why bf16 has fp32's range. fp32 is laid out [1 sign | 8 exponent | 23 mantissa]. Its top 16 bits are [1 | 8 | 7]: exactly bf16. So cutting the low 16 bits keeps $s$ and $e$ untouched and only shortens $m$. Same $k=8$, same bias, so Steps 1 and 2 give the same exponent range; only $p$ drops from 23 to 7. (Going back, bf16 $\to$ fp32 just appends 16 zero bits: exact.)

Example

Significant digits: $p+1$ bits (with the hidden 1) is $(p+1)\log_{10}2$ decimal digits.

$$\text{bf16: }8\cdot0.301\approx2.4,\qquad \text{fp16: }11\cdot0.301\approx3.3,\qquad \text{fp32: }24\cdot0.301\approx7.2.$$

So bf16 keeps under 3 digits: $256+1=256$. By Step 3 the gap at $256=2^8$ is $2^8\cdot2^{-7}=2$, so the neighbours are $256$ and $258$. $257$ sits exactly halfway; round-to-nearest-even picks the one with last mantissa bit 0, which is $256$.

Why bf16 for training

Activations and gradients span many orders of magnitude. bf16 has fp32's range, so nothing overflows or flushes to zero; fp16's narrow range does both.

fp16above 65504 → inf; tiny gradients → 0needs loss scaling
bf16same range as fp32no scaling; coarse precision is tolerated

How wide is each range? Smallest normal is the lowest exponent with mantissa $0$, $2^{1-\text{bias}}$; largest is from Step 2. In powers of ten:

$$\text{fp16: }\log_{10}\frac{65504}{2^{-14}}\approx9\ \text{decades},\qquad \text{bf16: }\log_{10}\frac{3.4\times10^{38}}{2^{-126}}\approx76\ \text{decades}.$$

Loss scaling shifts fp16's 9 decades to where the gradients live. Multiply the loss by $S$ (e.g. $2^{16}$). Differentiation is linear and backprop is a chain of linear maps (backpropagation), so every gradient is scaled too:

$$\nabla_w(S\cdot L)=S\cdot\nabla_wL.$$

With $S=2^j$, multiplying and later dividing only adds and subtracts $j$ in the exponent field, so both are exact unless they overflow or underflow. Divide by $S$ in fp32 before the update and you get $\nabla_wL$ back, having lifted every tiny gradient $j$ binades up out of the underflow zone. If an $\infty$ appears, $S$ was too big: skip that step and lower $S$.

Mixed precision: low precision where it is cheap

Do the bulk math (matmuls) in bf16 on tensor cores; keep anything that adds up many small numbers in fp32.

matmul inputsbf16
matmul accumulationfp32 (inside the tensor core)
master weights, optimizer statefp32
softmax, norms, lossfp32 (autocast chooses this)

Example: why fp32 master weights

$w=1$, update $\eta g=10^{-6}$. By Step 3, a sum moves to the next representable number only if the update exceeds half the gap at $w$:

$$\text{bf16: }\tfrac12\cdot2^{-7}=2^{-8}\approx3.9\times10^{-3}\gt10^{-6}\ \Rightarrow\ 1+10^{-6}\text{ rounds to }1.$$

$$\text{fp32: }\tfrac12\cdot2^{-23}=2^{-24}\approx6\times10^{-8}\lt10^{-6}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{the update survives}.$$

In bf16 every such step is lost and training silently stalls; fp32 master weights keep it.

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
model = Net().cuda()                                # weights stay fp32
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
for x, y in loader:
    x, y = x.cuda(), y.cuda()
    with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.bfloat16):
        loss = F.cross_entropy(model(x), y)         # matmuls run in bf16
    loss.backward()                                 # grads are fp32, like the weights
    opt.step(); opt.zero_grad()

For fp16, add scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda") and use scaler.scale(loss).backward(), scaler.step(opt), scaler.update().

Work it out yourself

What is the smallest positive normal number in fp16 and in bf16? A gradient of $10^{-8}$ is cast to fp16: what happens, and how does $S=2^{16}$ rescue it?

Solution

Smallest normal: exponent field $e=1$, mantissa $0$, so $1.m=1$:

$$x_{\min}=1\cdot2^{1-\text{bias}}.$$

$$\text{fp16: }2^{1-15}=2^{-14}\approx6.1\times10^{-5},\qquad \text{bf16: }2^{1-127}=2^{-126}\approx1.2\times10^{-38}.$$

Below that, $e=0$ means subnormal: $0.m\times2^{1-\text{bias}}$ (no hidden 1). The smallest has only the last mantissa bit set:

$$\text{fp16: }2^{-10}\cdot2^{-14}=2^{-24}\approx5.96\times10^{-8}.$$

$10^{-8}$ lies between $0$ and $2^{-24}$, below the midpoint $2^{-25}\approx2.98\times10^{-8}$, so it rounds to $0$: the gradient vanishes. In bf16, $10^{-8}\gg2^{-126}$, an ordinary number.

Scaled:

$$10^{-8}\cdot2^{16}=6.55\times10^{-4}\ \ge\ 2^{-14}\approx6.1\times10^{-5},$$

a normal fp16 value with full 11-bit precision. Unscale in fp32 and the update is intact.
