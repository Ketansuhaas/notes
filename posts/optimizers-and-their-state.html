Optimizers and their memory

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran
pytorchoptimizersadammemory

Builds on: Modules and the training loop, Gradient descent on a quadratic · Leads to: Datasets and DataLoaders

An optimizer turns p.grad into a step. Smarter steps remember the past, and that memory is a full-size tensor per parameter.

SGD: step downhill

Move against the gradient. Nothing to remember.

Near $\theta$ the loss is close to its first-order Taylor expansion, with $g=\nabla L(\theta)$:

$$L(\theta+\Delta)\approx L(\theta)+g^T\Delta.$$

Among steps of a fixed length $\|\Delta\|=r$, Cauchy-Schwarz bounds the change:

$$g^T\Delta\ \ge\ -\|g\|\,\|\Delta\|=-\|g\|\,r,\quad\text{equality iff }\Delta=-\tfrac{r}{\|g\|}\,g.$$

So the steepest decrease is along $-g$. Fold the scale into a learning rate $\eta$:

$$\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,g. \qquad \text{State: none (uses only today's } g).$$

Momentum: a running direction

Keep a velocity that sums recent gradients. Consistent directions build speed; the zig-zag across a narrow valley (GD on a quadratic) cancels out.

$$v\leftarrow\mu v+g,\qquad \theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,v. \qquad \text{State: } v\ (\text{one tensor}).$$

Unroll it from $v_0=0$: each old gradient is multiplied by $\mu$ once per step since it arrived.

$$v_t=g_t+\mu g_{t-1}+\mu^2g_{t-2}+\dots=\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mu^{\,t-s}g_s.$$

Consistent direction, $g_s=g$: a geometric series.

$$v_t=g\,\frac{1-\mu^t}{1-\mu}\ \to\ \frac{g}{1-\mu}=10\,g\quad(\mu=0.9).$$

Zig-zag, $g_s=(-1)^sg$: look for a steady state $v_t=(-1)^tc$ and plug into the update:

$$(-1)^tc=\mu(-1)^{t-1}c+(-1)^tg\ \Rightarrow\ c=-\mu c+g\ \Rightarrow\ c=\frac{g}{1+\mu}\approx0.53\,g.$$

The steady direction is amplified $10\times$, the oscillation damped to about half: a ratio of $(1+\mu)/(1-\mu)=19$.

Adam: momentum plus a per-coordinate step size

Also track the average squared gradient. Coordinates with big, noisy gradients get small steps; quiet ones get big steps: a cheap diagonal preconditioner.

$$m\leftarrow\beta_1m+(1-\beta_1)g,\qquad v\leftarrow\beta_2v+(1-\beta_2)g^2,$$ $$\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon},\qquad \hat m=\frac{m}{1-\beta_1^t},\ \hat v=\frac{v}{1-\beta_2^t}.$$

Defaults $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, $\epsilon=10^{-8}$. Each piece, derived:

Step 1: $m$ and $v$ are weighted averages. Unroll $m$ from $m_0=0$ as with momentum:

$$m_t=(1-\beta_1)\sum_{s=1}^{t}\beta_1^{\,t-s}g_s.$$

The weights form a geometric series:

$$(1-\beta_1)\big(1+\beta_1+\dots+\beta_1^{t-1}\big)=1-\beta_1^t.$$

As $t\to\infty$ they sum to $1$: an exponential moving average (EMA). The weight on a gradient $k$ steps old is $(1-\beta)\beta^k$, so the average age is $\sum_k k(1-\beta)\beta^k=\beta/(1-\beta)$: about $9$ steps for $\beta_1=0.9$, $999$ for $\beta_2=0.999$. $v$ is the same EMA of $g^2$.

Step 2: the bias correction. Early on the weights sum to only $1-\beta_1^t$, so $m_t$ is pulled toward its starting $0$. If the $g_s$ share a mean $\mathbb E[g]$, linearity of expectation gives

$$\mathbb E[m_t]=(1-\beta_1)\sum_{s=1}^{t}\beta_1^{\,t-s}\,\mathbb E[g]=(1-\beta_1^t)\,\mathbb E[g].$$

Dividing by $1-\beta_1^t$ makes it unbiased: $\mathbb E[\hat m_t]=\mathbb E[g]$. Likewise $\mathbb E[v_t]=(1-\beta_2^t)\,\mathbb E[g^2]$, hence $\hat v$. At $t=1$:

$$m_1=0.1\,g_1,\quad v_1=0.001\,g_1^2\quad\Rightarrow\quad \hat m_1=g_1,\quad \hat v_1=g_1^2.$$

The first step is $\eta\,g_1/|g_1|=\pm\eta$ per coordinate. Without the hats it would be $\eta\cdot0.1/\sqrt{0.001}\approx3.2\,\eta$.

Step 3: why noisy coordinates get small steps. Once the hats have settled, per coordinate $\hat m\approx\mathbb E[g]=:\mu_g$ and $\hat v\approx\mathbb E[g^2]=\mu_g^2+\sigma_g^2$ (variance $=\mathbb E[g^2]-\mathbb E[g]^2$). So the step is

$$\eta\,\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v}}\approx\eta\,\frac{\mu_g}{\sqrt{\mu_g^2+\sigma_g^2}}\in[-\eta,\eta].$$

Noise-free ($\sigma_g=0$): a full $\pm\eta$. Mostly noise ($\sigma_g\gg|\mu_g|$): about $\eta\,\mu_g/\sigma_g$, small. Scaling $g$ by $c$ scales top and bottom by $|c|$, so the step size is set by $\eta$, not by the gradient's units: each coordinate divided by its own RMS gradient is the diagonal preconditioner.

Step 4: AdamW's decoupled weight decay. An L2 penalty $\tfrac\lambda2\|\theta\|^2$ in the loss adds $\lambda\theta$ to $g$, which Adam then divides by $\sqrt{\hat v}$: weights with large gradients would barely decay. AdamW applies the decay outside the Adam ratio, equally to every weight:

$$\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\lambda\theta=(1-\eta\lambda)\,\theta,\qquad\text{then the Adam step.}$$

With lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.1: factor $1-3\times10^{-5}$ per step, so over $10^4$ steps a weight with no gradient shrinks by $(1-3\times10^{-5})^{10^4}\approx e^{-0.3}\approx0.74$.

opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.1)
loss_fn(model(x), y).backward()
opt.step()                       # state is created on the first step
p = next(model.parameters())
opt.state[p].keys()              # dict_keys(['step', 'exp_avg', 'exp_avg_sq'])

The memory bill

Training holds far more than the weights. In fp32: 4 bytes weight + 4 grad + 8 for Adam's $m,v$ = 16 bytes per parameter, $4\times$ the model. Activations come on top.

Every term is a full-size tensor (same shape as $\theta$) of 4-byte floats, so count bytes per parameter and multiply by $N$:

$$\text{SGD: }\underbrace{4}_{\theta}+\underbrace{4}_{g}=8,\qquad \text{momentum: }8+\underbrace{4}_{v}=12,\qquad \text{Adam: }8+\underbrace{4}_{m}+\underbrace{4}_{v}=16.$$

$$N=10^9:\quad 16\text{ B}\times10^9=16\text{ GB};\qquad \frac{4}{16}=\tfrac14\text{ is the model},\ \frac{8}{16}=\tfrac12\text{ is }m,v.$$

Activations are separate: each layer saves its input for backward (Autograd), a tensor of about $B\cdot T\cdot d$ numbers for batch $B$, sequence $T$, width $d$. Roughly

$$\text{activations}\approx\text{layers}\times c\times B\,T\,d\times\text{bytes},$$

with $c$ the number of such tensors each layer saves. Hence "batch $\times$ sequence length $\times$ layers".

Work it out yourself

A 7B-parameter model trained with Adam. Memory before activations (a) all in fp32; (b) bf16 weights and grads, fp32 $m$ and $v$. Does either fit on one 80 GB GPU?

Solution

(a) Adam in fp32 is $4+4+4+4=16$ bytes per parameter:

$$16\text{ B}\times7\times10^9=112\times10^9\text{ B}=112\text{ GB}.$$

(b) bf16 weights and grads, fp32 $m,v$:

$$\underbrace{2}_{\theta}+\underbrace{2}_{g}+\underbrace{4}_{m}+\underbrace{4}_{v}=12\text{ B}\ \Rightarrow\ 12\times7\times10^9\text{ B}=84\text{ GB}.$$

But bf16 cannot hold the weights during training. It has 8 significant bits (7 stored + the implicit 1, about $\log_{10}2^8\approx2.4$ decimal digits), so in $[1,2)$ its numbers are spaced $2^{-7}\approx0.0078$ apart (in $[2^e,2^{e+1})$, $2^{e-7}$ apart; Floating point). Round-to-nearest drops any update $u$ smaller than half a spacing:

$$\theta\in[1,2),\ |u|\lt2^{-8}\ \Rightarrow\ \theta+u\ \text{rounds back to}\ \theta.$$

The cutoff there is $2^{-8}\approx0.0039$, while Adam's steps are at most about $\eta=3\times10^{-4}$ (Step 3): every one vanishes. Smaller weights tolerate smaller steps, but the smallest updates (noisy coordinates, decayed learning rates) are still lost. Mixed precision therefore keeps an fp32 master copy: $12+4=16$ bytes, back to $112$ GB. Mixed precision saves activation memory and time, not this bill.

$84\gt80$ and $112\gt80$: neither fits on one 80 GB GPU even before activations. Hence sharding weights, grads and optimizer state across GPUs (ZeRO, FSDP): split over $8$ GPUs, $112/8=14$ GB each.
