The derivation toolkit

Builds on: Reading and writing proofs, Convexity and Jensen, Matrix calculus · Leads to: Convergence rates, Smoothness and strong convexity

Convergence proofs look magical until you notice they reuse the same dozen moves. Learn the moves and every line of such a proof becomes "oh, that one".

kindmoves
rewrite (an equality)add and subtract, expand a square, complete the square, telescope, trace trick, FTC along a line
bound (an inequality)Cauchy-Schwarz, Young, Jensen, union bound, $1+x\le e^x$

Add and subtract

When: you must compare $x$ with $x^*$ but only know facts about how each relates to some $y$. Move: add $0=-y+y$.

$$x-x^*=(x-y)+(y-x^*).$$

Worked use: reverse triangle inequality

$$\|a\|=\|(a-b)+b\|\le\|a-b\|+\|b\| \quad(\text{add and subtract } b,\text{ then triangle inequality})$$ $$\Rightarrow\ \|a\|-\|b\|\le\|a-b\|.$$

Swapping $a,b$ gives the other sign, so $\big|\|a\|-\|b\|\big|\le\|a-b\|$.

Expand a square

When: a squared length of a sum or difference, e.g. distance to the optimum after one step. Move: multiply it out.

$$\|a-b\|^2=(a-b)^T(a-b)=a^Ta-a^Tb-b^Ta+b^Tb=\|a\|^2-2a^Tb+\|b\|^2,$$

using $b^Ta=a^Tb$ (a scalar equals its transpose).

Worked use: one gradient step

With $x_{k+1}=x_k-\eta g$, take $a=x_k-x^*$, $b=\eta g$:

$$\|x_{k+1}-x^*\|^2=\|x_k-x^*\|^2-2\eta\,g^T(x_k-x^*)+\eta^2\|g\|^2.$$

This is the first line of the convex gradient descent proof. Check on $f=\tfrac12x^TAx$, $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, $x^*=0$, $x_k=(1,0)$, $\eta=\tfrac13$: $g=Ax_k=(2,1)$, $x_{k+1}=(\tfrac13,-\tfrac13)$, so the left side is $\tfrac29$. Right side: $1-2\cdot\tfrac13\cdot2+\tfrac19\cdot5=\tfrac{9-12+5}{9}=\tfrac29$. ✓

Complete the square

When: a quadratic plus a linear term that you want to minimize (or integrate, as for Gaussians). Move: fold the linear term into a shifted square; the minimum is read off.

$$ax^2+bx=a\Big(x+\frac{b}{2a}\Big)^2-\frac{b^2}{4a} \quad(\text{expand: } a(x+\tfrac b{2a})^2=ax^2+bx+\tfrac{b^2}{4a}).$$

Example: $x^2-4x+1=(x-2)^2-3$, so the minimum is $-3$ at $x=2$.

Worked use: the best step on a quadratic upper model

Minimize $m(d)=g^Td+\tfrac L2\|d\|^2$ over $d$. Expanding $\tfrac L2\|d+\tfrac gL\|^2=\tfrac L2\|d\|^2+g^Td+\tfrac{\|g\|^2}{2L}$ gives

$$m(d)=\frac L2\Big\|d+\frac gL\Big\|^2-\frac{\|g\|^2}{2L}\ \ge\ -\frac{\|g\|^2}{2L},$$

with equality at $d=-g/L$: a gradient step of size $1/L$ that lowers the model by $\|g\|^2/2L$.

Telescoping sums

When: you have a bound for one step and want one for $K$ steps. Move: sum the per-step differences; everything in the middle cancels.

$$\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}(a_k-a_{k+1})=(a_0-a_1)+(a_1-a_2)+\cdots+(a_{K-1}-a_K)=a_0-a_K,$$

since each middle $a_j$ appears once with $+$ and once with $-$.

Worked use: average gradient size

Suppose each step decreases $f$ by at least $c\|g_k\|^2$, i.e. $f(x_k)-f(x_{k+1})\ge c\|g_k\|^2$. Sum over $k=0,\dots,K-1$:

$$c\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\|g_k\|^2\le f(x_0)-f(x_K)\le f(x_0)-f^* \quad(\text{telescope, then } f(x_K)\ge f^*).$$

The smallest term is at most the average, so $\min_k\|g_k\|^2\le\dfrac{f(x_0)-f^*}{cK}$. The same works for products: $\prod_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k}=\frac{a_K}{a_0}$.

Cauchy-Schwarz

When: an inner product (gradient $\cdot$ displacement) must be bounded by lengths you control. Move: $|a^Tb|\le\|a\|\,\|b\|$.

Proof. If $b=0$ both sides are $0$. Otherwise, for every $t$, expand a square, then pick the best $t=a^Tb/\|b\|^2$ (complete the square):

$$0\le\|a-tb\|^2=\|a\|^2-2t\,a^Tb+t^2\|b\|^2 \ \overset{t=a^Tb/\|b\|^2}{=}\ \|a\|^2-\frac{(a^Tb)^2}{\|b\|^2}.$$

Multiply by $\|b\|^2$ and take square roots: $|a^Tb|\le\|a\|\|b\|$. $\square$

Worked use

$a=(1,2)$, $b=(3,1)$: $a^Tb=5\le\sqrt5\sqrt{10}=\sqrt{50}\approx7.07$. In proofs: $g^T(x-x^*)\le\|g\|\,\|x-x^*\|$.

Young's inequality

When: a cross term $a^Tb$ is in the way, and you would rather have two squares you already control. Move: split it.

$$a^Tb\le\frac{\|a\|^2}{2}+\frac{\|b\|^2}{2} \quad\Leftarrow\quad 0\le\|a-b\|^2=\|a\|^2-2a^Tb+\|b\|^2.$$

Weighted version: apply it to $\sqrt\varepsilon\,a$ and $b/\sqrt\varepsilon$ to get $a^Tb\le\frac\varepsilon2\|a\|^2+\frac1{2\varepsilon}\|b\|^2$ for any $\varepsilon\gt0$.

Worked use: a squared sum

$$\|a+b\|^2=\|a\|^2+2a^Tb+\|b\|^2\le\|a\|^2+(\|a\|^2+\|b\|^2)+\|b\|^2=2\|a\|^2+2\|b\|^2.$$

In SGD, with gradient $g=\nabla f+\text{noise}$, this gives $\|g\|^2\le2\|\nabla f\|^2+2\|\text{noise}\|^2$.

Jensen's inequality

When: an average sits inside a convex function (or outside). Move: for convex $f$, $f(\mathbb{E}X)\le\mathbb{E}f(X)$.

Proof (from Convexity and Jensen). A convex $f$ lies above its tangent at $\mu=\mathbb{E}X$; take $\mathbb{E}$ and use $\mathbb{E}[X-\mu]=0$:

$$f(X)\ge f(\mu)+\nabla f(\mu)^T(X-\mu) \ \Rightarrow\ \mathbb{E}f(X)\ge f(\mu).\qquad\square$$

Worked use: the averaged iterate

Let $X$ be one of $x_0,\dots,x_{K-1}$ picked uniformly, so $\mathbb{E}X=\bar x=\frac1K\sum_kx_k$. Jensen gives

$$f(\bar x)\le\frac1K\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}f(x_k).$$

So a bound on the average of $f(x_k)$ (which telescoping produces) becomes a bound for one point, $\bar x$.

The trace trick

When: the expectation of a quadratic form, or a gradient with respect to a matrix. Move: a scalar is its own trace, and the trace is cyclic.

$$s=\operatorname{tr}(s), \qquad \operatorname{tr}(AB)=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ji}=\operatorname{tr}(BA).$$

Worked use: $\mathbb{E}[x^TAx]$ for $x$ with mean $\mu$, covariance $\Sigma$

$$\mathbb{E}[x^TAx]=\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(x^TAx)=\mathbb{E}\operatorname{tr}(Axx^T) \quad(\text{scalar} = \text{trace, cyclic})$$ $$=\operatorname{tr}(A\,\mathbb{E}[xx^T]) \quad(\text{trace and }\mathbb{E}\text{ are both linear})$$ $$=\operatorname{tr}(A\Sigma)+\mu^TA\mu \quad(\mathbb{E}[xx^T]=\Sigma+\mu\mu^T).$$

The last fact: expand $\Sigma=\mathbb{E}[(x-\mu)(x-\mu)^T]=\mathbb{E}[xx^T]-\mu\mu^T$. With $A=I$, $\mu=0$: the expected squared noise in SGD is $\mathbb{E}\|\xi\|^2=\operatorname{tr}\Sigma$. More in Matrix calculus.

Taylor with remainder: FTC along a line

When: you know something about $\nabla f$ everywhere (e.g. it is Lipschitz) and need a statement about $f$ itself. Move: walk from $x$ to $y$ in a straight line and add up the slopes.

Let $d=y-x$ and $\varphi(t)=f(x+td)$, so $\varphi(0)=f(x)$, $\varphi(1)=f(y)$. The chain rule gives $\varphi'(t)=\nabla f(x+td)^Td$, and the fundamental theorem of calculus gives

$$f(y)=f(x)+\int_0^1\nabla f(x+td)^T d\,dt.$$

Worked use: the quadratic upper bound

Assume $\|\nabla f(u)-\nabla f(v)\|\le L\|u-v\|$. Subtract $\nabla f(x)^Td=\int_0^1\nabla f(x)^Td\,dt$ from both sides:

$$f(y)-f(x)-\nabla f(x)^Td=\int_0^1\big(\nabla f(x+td)-\nabla f(x)\big)^Td\,dt$$ $$\le\int_0^1 \|\nabla f(x+td)-\nabla f(x)\|\,\|d\|\,dt \quad(\text{Cauchy-Schwarz})$$ $$\le\int_0^1 Lt\|d\|^2\,dt=\frac L2\|d\|^2 \quad(\text{Lipschitz, then }\textstyle\int_0^1t\,dt=\tfrac12).$$

So $f(y)\le f(x)+\nabla f(x)^T(y-x)+\frac L2\|y-x\|^2$, the model minimized in "complete the square" above. For the running quadratic, $\nabla f=Ax$ has $L=\lambda_{\max}=3$.

Union bound

When: you need many bad events to all be avoided at once (every step, every coordinate). Move: the chance that any happens is at most the sum of their chances.

$$P\Big(\bigcup_iE_i\Big)\le\sum_iP(E_i).$$

Proof. Pointwise, $\mathbf 1[\text{some } E_i]\le\sum_i\mathbf 1[E_i]$: the left side is $0$ or $1$, and when it is $1$ at least one term on the right is $1$. Take $\mathbb{E}$ of both sides. $\square$

Worked use

Each of $K=100$ steps fails with probability at most $10^{-4}$. Then $P(\text{any step fails})\le100\cdot10^{-4}=10^{-2}$, no independence needed. Pairs with tail bounds in Concentration inequalities.

$1+x\le e^x$

When: converting a per-step contraction $(1-a)^k$ into an iteration count. Move: the line $1+x$ is the tangent of the convex $e^x$ at $0$, so it lies below.

Proof. $h(x)=e^x-1-x$ has $h'(x)=e^x-1$ (zero at $x=0$) and $h''(x)=e^x\gt0$, so $h$ is convex with minimum $h(0)=0$. Hence $h\ge0$. $\square$

For $0\le a\le1$ both $1-a$ and $e^{-a}$ are $\ge0$, so raising $1-a\le e^{-a}$ to the $k$-th power keeps the order:

$$(1-a)^k\le e^{-ak}.$$

Worked use: how tight is it?

$a$, $k$$(1-a)^k$$e^{-ak}$
$0.01$, $100$$0.366$$e^{-1}=0.368$ (nearly exact)
$0.5$, $10$$0.00098$$e^{-5}=0.0067$ (loose but safe)

Small $a$ (slow, ill-conditioned problems) is exactly where the bound is tight, and where iteration counts matter.

Derive it yourself

An error $e_k\gt0$ contracts every step: $e_{k+1}\le(1-\tfrac1\kappa)\,e_k$ with $\kappa\gt1$. Using telescoping and $1+x\le e^x$, show $e_k\le\varepsilon\,e_0$ once $k\ge\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon)$. How many steps for $\kappa=3$, $\varepsilon=10^{-6}$?

Solution

Want: $\log e_k-\log e_0\le\log\varepsilon$. Key step: telescope the logs.

$$\log e_k-\log e_0=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\big(\log e_{j+1}-\log e_j\big) \quad(\text{telescoping})$$ $$\le\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\log\Big(1-\frac1\kappa\Big)=k\log\Big(1-\frac1\kappa\Big) \quad(\text{each step contracts}).$$

Take $\log$ of $1+x\le e^x$ with $x=-\tfrac1\kappa$ (allowed: $1-\tfrac1\kappa\gt0$ as $\kappa\gt1$):

$$\log\Big(1-\frac1\kappa\Big)\le-\frac1\kappa \ \Rightarrow\ \log\frac{e_k}{e_0}\le-\frac k\kappa.$$

If $k\ge\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon)$ then $-\tfrac k\kappa\le-\log(1/\varepsilon)=\log\varepsilon$, so $\log\frac{e_k}{e_0}\le\log\varepsilon$, i.e. $e_k\le\varepsilon e_0$. $\square$

Numbers. $\kappa\log(1/\varepsilon)=3\cdot\log10^6=3\cdot13.82=41.4$, so $k=42$ suffices. Check: $(\tfrac23)^{42}=e^{42\log(2/3)}=e^{-17.0}\approx4\times10^{-8}\le10^{-6}$. ✓ (The exact need is $\log10^6/\log1.5=34.1$, so $35$ steps: the bound is safe, a little generous.)
