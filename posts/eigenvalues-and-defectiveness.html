Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Most vectors get turned by $A$. Eigenvectors are the special directions it only stretches; $\lambda$ is the stretch factor.

$$\underbrace{A}_{n\times n}\underbrace{v}_{n\times1}=\lambda\underbrace{v}_{n\times1},\ v\neq0 \iff N(A-\lambda I)\neq\{0\} \iff \det(A-\lambda I)=0.$$

Example

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}: \ \lambda^2-4\lambda+3=0 \Rightarrow \lambda=3,1. \qquad \lambda=3: v=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}, \quad \lambda=1: v=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.$$

Diagonalization

With $n$ independent eigenvectors, use them as coordinates. In those coordinates $A$ is a pure scaling.

$$A\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}v_1&\cdots&v_n\end{pmatrix}}_{V,\ n\times n}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1v_1&\cdots&\lambda_nv_n\end{pmatrix}=V\underbrace{\Lambda}_{\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i)}\quad(\text{always}).$$

$$A=V\Lambda V^{-1} \iff V \text{ invertible} \iff \text{the eigenvectors are independent}.$$

Example

$V=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $\det V=-2\neq0$, so $A=V\Lambda V^{-1}$ with $\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(3,1)$.

Algebraic vs. geometric multiplicity

AM counts how often $\lambda$ appears as a root on paper. GM counts how many independent directions actually get it. GM can fall short.

$$\operatorname{AM}(\lambda)=\text{root multiplicity in }\det(A-\lambda I), \qquad \operatorname{GM}(\lambda)=\dim N(A-\lambda I),$$ $$1\le\operatorname{GM}\le\operatorname{AM}.$$

Example

$$\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}: (2-\lambda)^2 \Rightarrow \operatorname{AM}(2)=2. \quad A-2I=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix} \Rightarrow N=\operatorname{span}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\ \operatorname{GM}(2)=1.$$

Defectiveness

A repeated eigenvalue does not hand you extra eigenvectors for free. If one comes up short, there are too few eigenvectors for a basis.

$$\text{defective}\iff\operatorname{GM}(\lambda)\lt\operatorname{AM}(\lambda) \text{ for some } \lambda; \qquad \text{diagonalizable}\iff\operatorname{GM}=\operatorname{AM}\ \ \forall\lambda.$$

$\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$$\operatorname{AM}(2)=2$, $\operatorname{GM}(2)=1$defective
$\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$$\operatorname{AM}(2)=2$, $\operatorname{GM}(2)=2$fine (already diagonal)

Eigenvalues and rank

$\lambda=0$ marks the directions $A$ collapses. Everything else survives and counts toward rank.

$$N(A)=N(A-0I) \ \Rightarrow\ \operatorname{rank}A=n-\operatorname{GM}(0) \quad(\text{rank-nullity}).$$

If $A$ is diagonalizable, rank $=$ number of nonzero eigenvalues (with multiplicity). Not otherwise: $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ has rank 1, eigenvalues $0,0$.

Example

$$\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}: \ \lambda^2-5\lambda=0 \Rightarrow \lambda=0,5. \quad N(A)=\operatorname{span}\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\end{pmatrix}, \ \operatorname{rank}=2-1=1 = \#\{\lambda\neq0\}.$$

Derive it yourself

Show that eigenvectors $v_1,v_2$ with $\lambda_1\neq\lambda_2$ are independent. Why does this mean only a repeated eigenvalue can make $A$ defective?

Solution

Suppose $c_1v_1+c_2v_2=0$. Apply $A$: $c_1\lambda_1v_1+c_2\lambda_2v_2=0$. Subtract $\lambda_2$ times the first: $c_1(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)v_1=0\Rightarrow c_1=0$, then $c_2=0$. The same trick (by induction) gives $n$ independent eigenvectors when all $n$ eigenvalues differ, so $V$ is invertible.
