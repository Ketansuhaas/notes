Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Most vectors get turned by $A$. Eigenvectors are the special directions it only stretches; $\lambda$ is the stretch factor.

Move $\lambda v$ to the left ($\lambda v=\lambda Iv$):

$$\underbrace{A}_{n\times n}\underbrace{v}_{n\times1}=\lambda\underbrace{v}_{n\times1},\ v\neq0 \iff (A-\lambda I)v=0,\ v\neq0 \iff N(A-\lambda I)\neq\{0\}.$$

A square matrix has a nonzero null vector exactly when it is not invertible, and that is exactly when its determinant is $0$:

$$N(A-\lambda I)\neq\{0\} \iff \det(A-\lambda I)=0 \quad\text{(the characteristic equation).}$$

Example

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}: \ \det\begin{pmatrix}2-\lambda&1\\1&2-\lambda\end{pmatrix}=(2-\lambda)^2-1=\lambda^2-4\lambda+3=(\lambda-3)(\lambda-1),$$

so $\lambda=3,1$. Each eigenvector is a null vector of $A-\lambda I$:

$$\lambda=3:\ \begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}v=0\ \Rightarrow\ v_1=v_2\ \Rightarrow\ v=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},$$

$$\lambda=1:\ \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}v=0\ \Rightarrow\ v_1=-v_2\ \Rightarrow\ v=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.$$

Diagonalization

With $n$ independent eigenvectors, use them as coordinates. In those coordinates $A$ is a pure scaling.

Column $i$ of $AV$ is $Av_i=\lambda_iv_i$ (view 2 of matrix multiplication). Column $i$ of $V\Lambda$ mixes $V$'s columns with weights $\text{col}_i(\Lambda)=\lambda_ie_i$, which is also $\lambda_iv_i$. So

$$A\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}v_1&\cdots&v_n\end{pmatrix}}_{V,\ n\times n}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1v_1&\cdots&\lambda_nv_n\end{pmatrix}=V\underbrace{\Lambda}_{\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_i)}\quad(\text{always}).$$

If $V$ is invertible, multiply on the right by $V^{-1}$. A square $V$ is invertible exactly when its columns are independent (rank $n$):

$$A=V\Lambda V^{-1} \iff V \text{ invertible} \iff \text{the eigenvectors are independent}.$$

Example

$V=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $\det V=(1)(-1)-(1)(1)=-2\neq0$. The $2\times2$ inverse swaps the diagonal, negates the off-diagonal, and divides by $\det$:

$$V^{-1}=\tfrac{1}{-2}\begin{pmatrix}-1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad V\Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\3&-1\end{pmatrix},$$

$$V\Lambda V^{-1}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}3+1&3-1\\3-1&3+1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}=A. \ \checkmark$$

Reading $A=V\Lambda V^{-1}$: scale the eigen-components

Split $x$ into pieces along the eigenvectors. $A$ just multiplies each piece by its own eigenvalue. The pieces never mix.

Step 1: the components. Write $x$ as a mix of eigenvectors (view 2: $Vc$ is that mix), and solve for the weights:

$$x=c_1v_1+\cdots+c_nv_n=Vc \quad\Rightarrow\quad c=V^{-1}x.$$

Step 2: apply $A$. Use linearity, then $Av_i=\lambda_iv_i$:

$$Ax=\sum_ic_i\,Av_i=\sum_i(\lambda_ic_i)\,v_i.$$

Each eigen-component $c_i$ is rescaled to $\lambda_ic_i$ and stays on its own axis. So $V\Lambda V^{-1}$, read right to left, is three steps:

$V^{-1}x$find the eigen-components $c_i$
$\Lambda\,(\cdot)$scale each one: $c_i\to\lambda_ic_i$ (no mixing)
$V\,(\cdot)$rebuild the vector from the scaled pieces

Repeating is free: applying Step 2 $k$ times gives $A^kx=\sum_i\lambda_i^kc_i\,v_i$ (see Dynamics in the eigenbasis).

The catch: components are parallel slides, not shadows

If the eigenvectors aren't perpendicular, you find $c_i$ by sliding $x$ parallel to the other eigenvectors until it hits axis $i$. That is what $V^{-1}x$ computes. Dropping a perpendicular shadow (a dot product) gives the wrong answer.

Example: a non-symmetric $A$

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&3\end{pmatrix}: \quad \det(A-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)(3-\lambda)\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda=2,3.$$

$$\lambda=2:\ \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}v=0\Rightarrow v_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}; \qquad \lambda=3:\ \begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}v=0\Rightarrow v_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.$$

They are not perpendicular: $v_1\cdot v_2=1$. Take $x=(3,2)$.

Parallel slides ($V^{-1}x$).

$$V=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\ \ V^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \ \Rightarrow\ c=V^{-1}x=\begin{pmatrix}3-2\\2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\end{pmatrix}.$$

Rebuild: $1\cdot(1,0)+2\cdot(1,1)=(3,2)=x$. ✓ Then scale: $Ax=2\cdot1\cdot(1,0)+3\cdot2\cdot(1,1)=(8,6)$, and directly $Ax=(2\cdot3+1\cdot2,\ 3\cdot2)=(8,6)$. ✓

Perpendicular shadows (dot products). The shadow coefficient of $x$ on $v$ is $x\cdot v/\|v\|^2$:

$$\frac{x\cdot v_1}{\|v_1\|^2}=\frac{3}{1}=3, \qquad \frac{x\cdot v_2}{\|v_2\|^2}=\frac{5}{2}=2.5 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 3\,v_1+2.5\,v_2=(5.5,\ 2.5)\neq x. \ \ \text{✗}$$

The shadows double-count: $v_1$ and $v_2$ overlap ($v_1\cdot v_2\neq0$), so part of $x$ is credited to both.

When the eigenvectors are orthonormal (every symmetric $A$, by the spectral theorem), the two methods agree: $V^{-1}=V^T$, so the slide becomes a shadow.

$$V^TV=I\ \Rightarrow\ V^{-1}=V^T\ \Rightarrow\ c=V^Tx,\ \ c_i=q_i^Tx.$$

This is why the SVD and PCA can use plain dot products: their axes are perpendicular by construction.

Algebraic vs. geometric multiplicity

AM counts how often $\lambda$ appears as a root on paper. GM counts how many independent directions actually get it. GM can fall short.

$$\operatorname{AM}(\lambda)=\text{root multiplicity in }\det(A-\lambda I), \qquad \operatorname{GM}(\lambda)=\dim N(A-\lambda I),$$ $$1\le\operatorname{GM}\le\operatorname{AM}.$$

Why $\operatorname{GM}\ge1$. $\lambda$ is a root, so $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$, so $N(A-\lambda I)$ has a nonzero vector.

Why $\operatorname{GM}\le\operatorname{AM}$. Let $g=\operatorname{GM}(\lambda_0)$. Put $g$ independent eigenvectors first in a basis $S=(v_1,\dots,v_g,\dots)$. Column $i\le g$ of $AS$ is $\lambda_0v_i$, so

$$S^{-1}AS=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_0I_g&\ast\\0&C\end{pmatrix}.$$

Changing basis does not change the characteristic polynomial, since $\det(S^{-1})\det(S)=1$:

$$\det(S^{-1}AS-tI)=\det\big(S^{-1}(A-tI)S\big)=\det(A-tI).$$

The block-triangular determinant is the product of the diagonal blocks' determinants:

$$\det(A-tI)=(\lambda_0-t)^g\det(C-tI) \ \Rightarrow\ \operatorname{AM}(\lambda_0)\ge g.$$

Example

$$\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}: \ \det\begin{pmatrix}2-\lambda&1\\0&2-\lambda\end{pmatrix}=(2-\lambda)^2-1\cdot0=(2-\lambda)^2 \ \Rightarrow\ \operatorname{AM}(2)=2.$$

$$A-2I=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}:\ (A-2I)v=\begin{pmatrix}v_2\\0\end{pmatrix}=0 \iff v_2=0 \ \Rightarrow\ N=\operatorname{span}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix},\ \operatorname{GM}(2)=1.$$

Defectiveness

A repeated eigenvalue does not hand you extra eigenvectors for free. If one comes up short, there are too few eigenvectors for a basis.

$$\text{defective}\iff\operatorname{GM}(\lambda)\lt\operatorname{AM}(\lambda) \text{ for some } \lambda; \qquad \text{diagonalizable}\iff\operatorname{GM}=\operatorname{AM}\ \ \forall\lambda.$$

Why. Count over all distinct eigenvalues (complex ones included). The characteristic polynomial has degree $n$, so its roots fill $n$ slots, and each $\operatorname{GM}\le\operatorname{AM}$:

$$\sum_\lambda\operatorname{GM}(\lambda)\ \le\ \sum_\lambda\operatorname{AM}(\lambda)=n,$$

with equality iff $\operatorname{GM}=\operatorname{AM}$ for every $\lambda$. Gather a basis of each eigenspace. The combined list is independent: if $\sum_\lambda w_\lambda=0$ with $w_\lambda$ in eigenspace $\lambda$, the nonzero $w_\lambda$ would be eigenvectors with distinct eigenvalues summing to $0$, impossible by the exercise. So we hold exactly $\sum_\lambda\operatorname{GM}(\lambda)$ independent eigenvectors, and that is $n$ (a full $V$) iff $\operatorname{GM}=\operatorname{AM}$ everywhere.

$\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$$\operatorname{AM}(2)=2$, $\operatorname{GM}(2)=1$defective
$\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$$\operatorname{AM}(2)=2$, $\operatorname{GM}(2)=2$fine (already diagonal)

For $2I$: $\det(2I-\lambda I)=(2-\lambda)^2$, and $2I-2I=0$ kills every vector, so $N=\mathbb{R}^2$ and $\operatorname{GM}(2)=2$.

Eigenvalues and rank

$\lambda=0$ marks the directions $A$ collapses. Everything else survives and counts toward rank.

$$N(A)=N(A-0I) \ \Rightarrow\ \operatorname{rank}A=n-\operatorname{GM}(0) \quad(\text{rank-nullity}).$$

If $A$ is diagonalizable, $\operatorname{GM}(0)=\operatorname{AM}(0)$ (previous section), so

$$\operatorname{rank}A=n-\operatorname{AM}(0)=\#\{\text{nonzero eigenvalues, with multiplicity}\}.$$

Not otherwise: $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ has $\det=\lambda^2$, so eigenvalues $0,0$ ($\operatorname{AM}(0)=2$), yet its null space is only $\operatorname{span}(1,0)$ ($\operatorname{GM}(0)=1$), so rank $=2-1=1$, not $0$.

Example

$$\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}: \ (1-\lambda)(4-\lambda)-2\cdot2=\lambda^2-5\lambda+4-4=\lambda(\lambda-5) \ \Rightarrow\ \lambda=0,5.$$

$$N(A):\ x_1+2x_2=0\ \Rightarrow\ N(A)=\operatorname{span}\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\end{pmatrix}, \quad \operatorname{rank}=2-1=1=\#\{\lambda\neq0\}.$$

Derive it yourself

Show that eigenvectors $v_1,v_2$ with $\lambda_1\neq\lambda_2$ are independent. Why does this mean only a repeated eigenvalue can make $A$ defective?

Solution

Suppose $c_1v_1+c_2v_2=0$. Apply $A$, and separately multiply by $\lambda_2$:

$$c_1\lambda_1v_1+c_2\lambda_2v_2=0, \qquad c_1\lambda_2v_1+c_2\lambda_2v_2=0.$$

Subtract: $c_1(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)v_1=0$. Both $\lambda_1-\lambda_2\neq0$ and $v_1\neq0$, so $c_1=0$; then $c_2v_2=0$ gives $c_2=0$.

Any number, by induction. Say $v_1,\dots,v_k$ (distinct $\lambda$'s) are independent, and $\sum_{i\le k+1}c_iv_i=0$. Apply $A-\lambda_{k+1}I$, which sends $v_i\mapsto(\lambda_i-\lambda_{k+1})v_i$ and kills $v_{k+1}$:

$$\sum_{i\le k}c_i(\lambda_i-\lambda_{k+1})v_i=0 \ \Rightarrow\ c_i(\lambda_i-\lambda_{k+1})=0 \ \Rightarrow\ c_i=0\ \ (i\le k),$$

and then $c_{k+1}v_{k+1}=0$ gives $c_{k+1}=0$.

Why only repeats. If every eigenvalue is a simple root, $\operatorname{AM}=1$, and $1\le\operatorname{GM}\le\operatorname{AM}=1$ forces $\operatorname{GM}=\operatorname{AM}$: never defective. Equivalently, $n$ distinct eigenvalues give $n$ independent eigenvectors, so $V$ is invertible.
