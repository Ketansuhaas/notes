The reparameterization trick

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran
deep-learninggenerative-modelingreparameterization-trickgradients

Builds on: Generative modeling, Automatic differentiation, Expectation and variance algebra · Leads to: VAEs and diffusion (coming)

A VAE's loss averages over random samples whose distribution depends on the parameters. Move the randomness into fixed noise, and the gradient flows straight through the sample.

The problem

$\theta$ lives in the distribution, not in $f$. A sampled $z$ is just a number: autodiff sees no path from it back to $\theta$.

Write the expectation as an integral. $\theta$ appears only in the density, so the gradient lands on $q_\theta$:

$$\nabla_\theta\,\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\theta}[f(z)]=\nabla_\theta\int f(z)\,q_\theta(z)\,dz=\int f(z)\,\nabla_\theta q_\theta(z)\,dz.$$

The naive estimate (sample $z$, differentiate $f$) gives zero, since $f(z)$ has no $\theta$:

$$\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\theta}[\nabla_\theta f(z)]=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\theta}[0]=0\ \ne\ \int f\,\nabla_\theta q_\theta\,dz.$$

And the correct integral is not an average under $q_\theta$: its weight $\nabla_\theta q_\theta$ integrates to $\nabla_\theta\int q_\theta=\nabla_\theta1=0$, so it is negative somewhere and is not a density. Sampling $z\sim q_\theta$ and averaging $f$ doesn't estimate it.

The trick: $z=\mu+\sigma\epsilon$

Write the sample as a deterministic function of $\theta$ and noise with no $\theta$ in it. The randomness moves to $\epsilon$; $\theta$ moves into the function.

$$z=\mu+\sigma\epsilon,\quad \epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1), \qquad \theta=(\mu,\sigma),\ \sigma\gt0.$$

It has the right distribution. Compute its CDF ($\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF), dividing by $\sigma\gt0$ keeps the inequality:

$$P(z\le t)=P(\mu+\sigma\epsilon\le t)=P\Big(\epsilon\le\frac{t-\mu}{\sigma}\Big)=\Phi\Big(\frac{t-\mu}{\sigma}\Big),$$

which is the CDF of $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$. So $z\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$. (Check: $\mathbb{E}[z]=\mu+\sigma\cdot0=\mu$, $\operatorname{Var}(z)=\sigma^2\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon)=\sigma^2$.)

The estimator is unbiased. Step 1: $z$ and $\mu+\sigma\epsilon$ have the same distribution, so they have the same expectation of $f$, and $\theta$ has moved out of the distribution into the integrand:

$$\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\theta}[f(z)]=\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}[f(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)]=\int f(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)\,p(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon.$$

Step 2: $p(\epsilon)$ has no $\theta$, so the gradient passes inside the integral (differentiation under the integral sign; fine when $f$ is smooth and $\nabla_\theta f$ is dominated by an integrable function) and lands on $f$:

$$\nabla_\theta\int f(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)\,p(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon=\int\nabla_\theta f(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)\,p(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon=\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon}\big[\nabla_\theta f(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)\big].$$

Step 3: this is an average under a fixed distribution, so sample it. By linearity of expectation each term has mean equal to the target, hence so does their average:

$$\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac1S\sum_{s=1}^S\nabla_\theta f(\mu+\sigma\epsilon_s)\Big]=\frac1S\sum_{s=1}^S\mathbb{E}_\epsilon\big[\nabla_\theta f(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)\big]=\nabla_\theta\,\mathbb{E}_{q_\theta}[f(z)].$$

Each sample's gradient is ordinary autodiff, the chain rule through $z=\mu+\sigma\epsilon$ with $\partial z/\partial\mu=1$, $\partial z/\partial\sigma=\epsilon$:

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial\mu}=f'(z)\cdot1=f'(z),\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial\sigma}=f'(z)\cdot\epsilon.$$

In a VAE the encoder outputs $\mu,\sigma$, applied per coordinate: $\ \underbrace{z}_{d\times1}=\underbrace{\mu_\phi(x)}_{d\times1}+\underbrace{\sigma_\phi(x)}_{d\times1}\odot\underbrace{\epsilon}_{d\times1}$.

General recipe: any $z=g_\theta(\epsilon)$ with $\epsilon\sim p(\epsilon)$ free of $\theta$; Steps 1 to 3 go through unchanged.

Example: $f(z)=z^2$

Exact. $\mathbb{E}[z^2]=\operatorname{Var}(z)+\mathbb{E}[z]^2=\sigma^2+\mu^2$, so $\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\mathbb{E}[z^2]=2\mu$.

Pathwise. Differentiate one sample, then average using $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon]=0$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)^2=2(\mu+\sigma\epsilon), \qquad \mathbb{E}\big[2(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)\big]=2\mu+2\sigma\cdot0=2\mu. \ \checkmark$$

One sample at $\mu=1,\ \sigma=0.5,\ \epsilon=0.4$: $2(1+0.5\cdot0.4)=2\cdot1.2=2.4$, versus the true $2$. Noisy, but unbiased. Its variance: $\operatorname{Var}\big(2\mu+2\sigma\epsilon\big)=4\sigma^2=1$.

The alternative: score function (REINFORCE)

Without a reparameterization, differentiate the density instead. The identity $\nabla q=q\,\nabla\log q$ turns the bad integral back into an average under $q$.

The derivative of a log: $\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta=\nabla_\theta q_\theta/q_\theta$, so $\nabla_\theta q_\theta=q_\theta\,\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta$. Substitute into the integral from "The problem":

$$\nabla_\theta\,\mathbb{E}_{q_\theta}[f(z)]=\int f(z)\,\nabla_\theta q_\theta(z)\,dz=\int f(z)\,q_\theta(z)\,\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(z)\,dz$$ $$=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim q_\theta}\big[f(z)\,\nabla_\theta\log q_\theta(z)\big].$$

Now an average under $q_\theta$ again, so sampling $z$ estimates it, with no derivative of $f$ needed.

Same example, score function

For $\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, $\log q=-\frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}+\text{const}$, so $\partial_\mu\log q=\frac{z-\mu}{\sigma^2}=\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma}$. Using $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon]=\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^3]=0$, $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^2]=1$:

$$\mathbb{E}\Big[(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)^2\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma}\Big]=\frac1\sigma\mathbb{E}\big[\mu^2\epsilon+2\mu\sigma\epsilon^2+\sigma^2\epsilon^3\big]=\frac{2\mu\sigma}{\sigma}=2\mu. \ \checkmark$$

Same mean, much more noise. Square the estimator and use $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^4]=3$, $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^6]=15$ (odd powers vanish):

$$\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big((\mu+\sigma\epsilon)^2\tfrac{\epsilon}{\sigma}\Big)^2\Big]=\frac{\mu^4+18\mu^2\sigma^2+15\sigma^4}{\sigma^2}$$ $$\Rightarrow\ \operatorname{Var}=\frac{\mu^4}{\sigma^2}+14\mu^2+15\sigma^2.$$

At $\mu=1,\ \sigma=0.5$: $\frac{1}{0.25}+14+15\cdot0.25=4+14+3.75=21.75$, versus $1$ for pathwise.

Why the score function is noisier. The score has mean zero, $\mathbb{E}_q[\nabla\log q]=\int\nabla q=\nabla1=0$. So shifting $f$ by a constant $c$ leaves the estimator's mean unchanged but adds $c\,\nabla\log q$, raising its variance by $c^2\operatorname{Var}(\nabla\log q)$. The estimator weighs $f$'s value, offset and all; the pathwise one uses only $f$'s slope, which ignores offsets. (This is also why baselines, subtracting a $c$ near $\mathbb{E}[f]$, help REINFORCE.)

Derive it yourself

Same example: $f(z)=z^2$, $z=\mu+\sigma\epsilon$. Find the pathwise estimator of $\partial\,\mathbb{E}[f]/\partial\sigma$ and show it is unbiased.

Solution

Chain rule through the sample, with $\partial z/\partial\sigma=\epsilon$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)^2=2(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)\cdot\epsilon=2\mu\epsilon+2\sigma\epsilon^2.$$

Its mean, by linearity, with $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon]=0$ and $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^2]=\operatorname{Var}(\epsilon)+0^2=1$:

$$\mathbb{E}[2\mu\epsilon+2\sigma\epsilon^2]=2\mu\cdot0+2\sigma\cdot1=2\sigma.$$

Exact: $\mathbb{E}[z^2]=\mu^2+\sigma^2$, so $\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}(\mu^2+\sigma^2)=2\sigma$. They agree, so the estimator is unbiased. ✓
