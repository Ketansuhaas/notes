Primal and dual problems

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran
linear-algebraoptimizationlagrangianconstrained-optimizationduality

Builds on: The Lagrangian and KKT conditions, Convexity and Jensen's inequality · Leads to: the SVM dual and kernel methods (beyond these notes)

Every choice of multipliers gives a lower bound on the constrained minimum. The dual problem hunts for the best bound; for convex problems it is exact.

The primal

The original constrained problem. Its Lagrangian charges a price for each constraint: $\mu_j\ge0$ per inequality, $\lambda_i$ per equality.

$$p^\star=\min_x f(x)\ \text{ s.t. } h_j(x)\le0,\ g_i(x)=0, \qquad L(x,\mu,\lambda)=f(x)+\mu^Th(x)+\lambda^Tg(x).$$

Running example

$\min x^2+y^2$ s.t. $h=1-x-y\le0$. From KKT: $x^\star=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, $p^\star=\tfrac12$, $\mu^\star=1$.

The dual function

Fix the prices, then minimize over $x$ with no constraints at all. The result is always concave, whatever $f$ is: a minimum of straight lines bends down.

$$d(\mu,\lambda)=\min_x L(x,\mu,\lambda) \qquad (\text{a min over } x \text{ of functions affine in } (\mu,\lambda)\ \Rightarrow\ \text{concave}).$$

Example

$$L=x^2+y^2+\mu(1-x-y), \quad \nabla_{x,y}L=0:\ x=y=\tfrac\mu2$$ $$\Rightarrow\ d(\mu)=\tfrac{\mu^2}4+\tfrac{\mu^2}4+\mu(1-\mu)=\mu-\tfrac{\mu^2}2.$$

Weak duality: $d^\star\le p^\star$, always

On the feasible set the penalty terms are $\le0$, so $L$ underestimates $f$. Every $\mu\ge0$ gives a lower bound; the dual asks for the best one.

$$x \text{ feasible},\ \mu\ge0: \qquad d(\mu,\lambda)\le L(x,\mu,\lambda)=f(x)+\underbrace{\mu^Th(x)}_{\le0}+\underbrace{\lambda^Tg(x)}_{=0}\le f(x)$$

$$\Rightarrow\quad d^\star=\max_{\mu\ge0,\,\lambda}\ d(\mu,\lambda)\ \le\ p^\star.$$

Example

$\mu$$0$$0.5$$1$$2$
$d(\mu)$$0$$0.375$$0.5$$0$

Every bound is $\le p^\star=\tfrac12$.

Strong duality: the gap closes

For a convex problem with one strictly feasible point, the best lower bound is exact. The optimal $(x^\star,\mu^\star,\lambda^\star)$ then satisfy KKT.

$$f,h_j \text{ convex},\ g_i \text{ affine},\ \exists x:\ h_j(x)\lt0\ \forall j\ \ (\text{Slater}) \quad\Rightarrow\quad d^\star=p^\star.$$

Example

$d'(\mu)=1-\mu=0$: $\mu^\star=1$, $d^\star=\tfrac12=p^\star$. Slater holds: $(1,1)$ has $h=-1\lt0$.

Why the dual is useful

The multipliers are prices, and the dual is sometimes the problem you can actually solve.

Shadow prices$\mu_j^\star$ = how fast $p^\star$ rises as constraint $j$ tightens. With $x+y\ge b\gt0$: $p^\star(b)=\tfrac{b^2}2$, $\ \tfrac{dp^\star}{db}=b=\mu^\star$.
SVMsThe dual sees the data only through $x_i^Tx_j$. Swap in a kernel $k(x_i,x_j)$ and get a nonlinear boundary for free.
Linear programsStrong duality holds whenever an optimum exists: max-flow = min-cut, the minimax theorem.

Derive it yourself

$A$ is $m\times n$ with independent rows. Derive the dual of $\min\tfrac12\|x\|^2$ s.t. $Ax=b$, solve it, and check $d^\star=p^\star$.

Solution

$L=\tfrac12\|x\|^2+\lambda^T(Ax-b)$. $\nabla_xL=x+A^T\lambda=0$, so $x=-A^T\lambda$ and $d(\lambda)=-\tfrac12\lambda^TAA^T\lambda-b^T\lambda$ (concave, since $AA^T\succ0$). Setting $\nabla d=0$: $\lambda^\star=-(AA^T)^{-1}b$, so $x^\star=A^T(AA^T)^{-1}b$ and $d^\star=\tfrac12b^T(AA^T)^{-1}b=\tfrac12\|x^\star\|^2=p^\star$. ✓ This $x^\star$ is the pseudoinverse solution $A^+b$ from the SVD.
