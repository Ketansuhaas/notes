Primal and dual problems

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran

Builds on: The Lagrangian and KKT conditions, Convexity and Jensen's inequality · Leads to: the SVM dual and kernel methods (beyond these notes)

Every choice of multipliers gives a lower bound on the constrained minimum. The dual problem hunts for the best bound; for convex problems it is exact.

The primal

The original constrained problem. Its Lagrangian charges a price for each constraint: $\mu_j\ge0$ per inequality, $\lambda_i$ per equality.

$$p^\star=\min_x f(x)\ \text{ s.t. } h_j(x)\le0,\ g_i(x)=0,$$

$$L(x,\mu,\lambda)=f(x)+\mu^Th(x)+\lambda^Tg(x).$$

Running example

$\min x^2+y^2$ s.t. $h=1-x-y\le0$. Solved in the KKT note: $x^\star=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, $p^\star=\tfrac14+\tfrac14=\tfrac12$, $\mu^\star=1$.

The dual function

Fix the prices, then minimize over $x$ with no constraints at all. The result is always concave, whatever $f$ is: a minimum of straight lines bends down.

$$d(\mu,\lambda)=\min_x L(x,\mu,\lambda).$$

Why concave. For each fixed $x$, $L$ is affine in $z=(\mu,\lambda)$, so it splits exactly over a mix $z=\theta a+(1-\theta)b$, $\theta\in[0,1]$:

$$L(x,\theta a+(1-\theta)b)=\theta L(x,a)+(1-\theta)L(x,b).$$

Minimize over $x$. Minimizing a sum gives at least the sum of the separate minima (each piece can pick its own $x$):

$$d(\theta a+(1-\theta)b)\ \ge\ \theta\min_xL(x,a)+(1-\theta)\min_xL(x,b)$$

$$=\theta\,d(a)+(1-\theta)\,d(b).$$

That is the chord test for concavity, with no assumption on $f$, $h$, $g$.

Example

$$L=x^2+y^2+\mu(1-x-y).$$

For fixed $\mu$, $L$ is a convex bowl in $(x,y)$, so its minimum is where the gradient vanishes:

$$\partial_xL=2x-\mu=0,\quad \partial_yL=2y-\mu=0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x=y=\tfrac\mu2.$$

Plug back in ($1-x-y=1-\mu$):

$$d(\mu)=\tfrac{\mu^2}4+\tfrac{\mu^2}4+\mu(1-\mu)=\tfrac{\mu^2}2+\mu-\mu^2=\mu-\tfrac{\mu^2}2.$$

A downward parabola: concave, as promised.

Weak duality: $d^\star\le p^\star$, always

On the feasible set the penalty terms are $\le0$, so $L$ underestimates $f$. Every $\mu\ge0$ gives a lower bound; the dual asks for the best one.

Take any feasible $x$ and any $\mu\ge0$, $\lambda$. The min over all $x'$ is at most the value at this $x$:

$$d(\mu,\lambda)\le L(x,\mu,\lambda)=f(x)+\underbrace{\mu^Th(x)}_{\le0:\ \mu\ge0,\ h\le0}+\underbrace{\lambda^Tg(x)}_{=0}\le f(x).$$

The left side doesn't depend on $x$, so minimize the right side over feasible $x$, then maximize the left over $\mu\ge0,\lambda$:

$$d(\mu,\lambda)\le p^\star \quad\Rightarrow\quad d^\star=\max_{\mu\ge0,\,\lambda}\ d(\mu,\lambda)\ \le\ p^\star.$$

Example: $d(\mu)=\mu-\mu^2/2$

$\mu$$0$$0.5$$1$$2$
$d(\mu)$$0-0=0$$0.5-0.125=0.375$$1-0.5=0.5$$2-2=0$

Every bound is $\le p^\star=\tfrac12$.

Strong duality: the gap closes

For a convex problem with one strictly feasible point, the best lower bound is exact. The optimal $(x^\star,\mu^\star,\lambda^\star)$ then satisfy KKT.

$$f,h_j \text{ convex},\ g_i \text{ affine},\ \exists x:\ h_j(x)\lt0\ \forall j\ \ (\text{Slater}) \quad\Rightarrow\quad d^\star=p^\star.$$

Proof idea (one inequality, no equalities). Collect every achievable pair $(u,t)$ with $u\ge h(x)$, $t\ge f(x)$ for some $x$. Convexity of $f,h$ makes this set convex, and $p^\star$ is the lowest $t$ it reaches over $u\le0$. A line of slope $-\mu$ that stays below the set meets the $t$-axis at height $\min_x\big(f(x)+\mu h(x)\big)=d(\mu)$. A convex set has a supporting line through its boundary point $(0,p^\star)$ (separating hyperplane theorem); Slater's point, with $u\lt0$, stops that line from being vertical, so its slope is a finite $-\mu^\star$ and $d(\mu^\star)=p^\star$. A full proof makes the separation step precise.

Then KKT holds. If $d^\star=p^\star$ with optimal $x^\star$ and $(\mu^\star,\lambda^\star)$, chain the weak duality steps at $x^\star$:

$$f(x^\star)=d(\mu^\star,\lambda^\star)=\min_xL(x,\mu^\star,\lambda^\star)$$

$$\le L(x^\star,\mu^\star,\lambda^\star)=f(x^\star)+\mu^{\star T}h(x^\star)+0\le f(x^\star).$$

Both ends are $f(x^\star)$, so both $\le$ are equalities. First: $x^\star$ minimizes $L(\cdot,\mu^\star,\lambda^\star)$, so $\nabla_xL=0$ (stationarity). Second: $\mu^{\star T}h(x^\star)=0$, a sum of terms each $\le0$, so every $\mu_j^\star h_j(x^\star)=0$ (complementary slackness).

Example

Maximize $d(\mu)=\mu-\mu^2/2$: $d'(\mu)=1-\mu=0$ gives $\mu^\star=1\ge0$, and $d^\star=1-\tfrac12=\tfrac12=p^\star$. Slater holds: $(1,1)$ has $h=1-1-1=-1\lt0$. And $\mu^\star=1$ is the KKT multiplier found before.

Why the dual is useful

The multipliers are prices, and the dual is sometimes the problem you can actually solve.

Shadow prices$\mu_j^\star$ = how fast $p^\star$ rises as constraint $j$ tightens. With $x+y\ge b\gt0$: $p^\star(b)=\tfrac{b^2}2$, $\ \tfrac{dp^\star}{db}=b=\mu^\star$.
SVMsThe dual sees the data only through $x_i^Tx_j$. Swap in a kernel $k(x_i,x_j)$ and get a nonlinear boundary for free.
Linear programsStrong duality holds whenever an optimum exists: max-flow = min-cut, the minimax theorem.

Shadow price, derived. Redo the dual with $h=b-x-y$. Stationarity again gives $x=y=\mu/2$, so

$$d(\mu;b)=\tfrac{\mu^2}2+\mu(b-\mu)=\mu b-\tfrac{\mu^2}2.$$

$\partial_\mu d=b-\mu=0$ gives $\mu^\star=b$, and by strong duality

$$p^\star(b)=d(b;b)=b^2-\tfrac{b^2}2=\tfrac{b^2}2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{dp^\star}{db}=b=\mu^\star.$$

Why in general: $b$ enters $L$ only through the term $\mu b$, and at the optimum $\partial_\mu d=0$, so moving $b$ changes $d^\star$ at rate $\mu^\star$ (envelope argument).

SVMs (proof idea): stationarity of the SVM Lagrangian in $w$ gives $w=\sum_i\alpha_iy_ix_i$, so every $w^Tw$ and $w^Tx$ in the dual becomes a sum of $x_i^Tx_j$ terms. Linear programs: a theorem of LP duality (proof via Farkas' lemma), stated here without proof.

Derive it yourself

$A$ is $m\times n$ with independent rows. Derive the dual of $\min\tfrac12\|x\|^2$ s.t. $Ax=b$, solve it, and check $d^\star=p^\star$.

Solution

Inner minimum. $L=\tfrac12\|x\|^2+\lambda^T(Ax-b)$ is a convex bowl in $x$, and $\nabla_xL=x+A^T\lambda$ (matrix calculus). Zero gives $x=-A^T\lambda$.

Dual function. Plug in, using $\|A^T\lambda\|^2=\lambda^TAA^T\lambda$:

$$d(\lambda)=\tfrac12\lambda^TAA^T\lambda+\lambda^T(-AA^T\lambda-b)$$

$$=-\tfrac12\lambda^TAA^T\lambda-b^T\lambda.$$

Concave, since $\lambda^TAA^T\lambda=\|A^T\lambda\|^2\gt0$ for $\lambda\ne0$ (independent rows means $A^T\lambda\ne0$), so $AA^T\succ0$ and is invertible. Write $M=(AA^T)^{-1}$.

Maximize. $\nabla d=-AA^T\lambda-b=0$:

$$\lambda^\star=-Mb, \qquad x^\star=-A^T\lambda^\star=A^TMb.$$

$$d^\star=-\tfrac12b^TM\,(AA^T)\,Mb+b^TMb=\tfrac12b^TMb.$$

Primal side. $x^\star$ is feasible: $Ax^\star=AA^TMb=b$. Its value:

$$\tfrac12\|x^\star\|^2=\tfrac12b^TMAA^TMb=\tfrac12b^TMb=d^\star.$$

Weak duality gives $d^\star\le p^\star\le\tfrac12\|x^\star\|^2=d^\star$, so $p^\star=d^\star$ and $x^\star$ is optimal. ✓

It is the pseudoinverse. With $A=U\Sigma V^T$ (SVD), $AA^T=U\,\Sigma\Sigma^T\,U^T$ with $\Sigma\Sigma^T=\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_i^2)$, so

$$A^TM=V\Sigma^TU^T\,U(\Sigma\Sigma^T)^{-1}U^T=V\,\Sigma^T(\Sigma\Sigma^T)^{-1}\,U^T.$$

$\Sigma^T(\Sigma\Sigma^T)^{-1}$ has $\sigma_i/\sigma_i^2=1/\sigma_i$ on its diagonal, which is $\Sigma^+$. So $x^\star=V\Sigma^+U^Tb=A^+b$.

Check with the running example as an equality, $A=(1\ \ 1)$, $b=1$: $AA^T=2$, $x^\star=\tfrac12(1,1)$, $d^\star=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\cdot1=\tfrac14$, which is $\tfrac12$ of $p^\star=\tfrac12$ because the objective here is halved. ✓
