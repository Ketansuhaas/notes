Variance decomposition and the eigenbasis

Builds on: The covariance matrix, intuitively, Principal component analysis · Leads to: Common distributions

The covariance note gave the spread along one ruler. Here: turn the ruler all the way round, and ask what a random ruler gets.

The variance profile

As the ruler turns, its spread traces a smooth wave between $\lambda_{\min}$ and $\lambda_{\max}$. Its weights are squared cosines to the axes.

$$\operatorname{Var}(u^Tx)=\sum_i\lambda_i\cos^2\theta_i,\quad \textstyle\sum_i\cos^2\theta_i=1. \qquad d=2:\ \ \lambda_1\cos^2\theta+\lambda_2\sin^2\theta.$$

From the covariance note: $\operatorname{Var}(u^Tx)=u^TCu=\sum_i\lambda_ic_i^2$ with $c_i=q_i^Tu$. Both are unit vectors, so the dot product is the cosine of the angle $\theta_i$ between $u$ and axis $q_i$:

$$c_i=q_i^Tu=\|q_i\|\|u\|\cos\theta_i=\cos\theta_i, \qquad \sum_i\cos^2\theta_i=\|c\|^2=\|u\|^2=1.$$

In $d=2$, $u$ at angle $\theta$ from $q_1$ is $90^\circ-\theta$ from $q_2$, so $\cos\theta_2=\sin\theta$. Using $\cos^2\theta=\tfrac{1+\cos2\theta}2$, $\sin^2\theta=\tfrac{1-\cos2\theta}2$, the profile is a pure wave:

$$\lambda_1\cos^2\theta+\lambda_2\sin^2\theta=\frac{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}2+\frac{\lambda_1-\lambda_2}2\cos2\theta \ \overset{3,\,1}{=}\ 2+\cos2\theta.$$

It swings between $2+1=3$ (at $\theta=0^\circ$) and $2-1=1$ (at $90^\circ$) around the mean $2$: the curve in the figure.

Example

$u=(1,0)$: $\cos\theta_1=q_1^Tu=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, so it sits at $45^\circ$, and $\cos^2\theta_1=\cos^2\theta_2=\tfrac12$:

$$3\cdot\tfrac12+1\cdot\tfrac12=2=C_{11}, \qquad\text{and } 2+\cos90^\circ=2. \ \checkmark$$

A random ruler gets the average

Point the ruler at random and you capture the mean eigenvalue. PCA's payoff is how far $\lambda_1$ sits above that.

$$u \text{ uniform on the unit sphere}: \qquad \mathbb{E}_u\big[u^TCu\big]=\frac{\operatorname{tr}C}{d}=\frac1d\sum_i\lambda_i.$$

In $d=2$ it is one line: a uniform direction means $\theta$ uniform on $[0,2\pi)$, and $\cos2\theta$ averages to $0$ over full periods:

$$\mathbb{E}_\theta\Big[\tfrac{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}2+\tfrac{\lambda_1-\lambda_2}2\cos2\theta\Big]=\frac{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}2.$$

General $d$: the exercise below. The last equality is $\operatorname{tr}C=\sum_i\lambda_i$ (covariance note).

Example

Average of the wave: $\tfrac{3+1}{2}=2$. PC1 gets $3$, i.e. $\tfrac32=1.5\times$ a random direction.

In $d=1000$ with one dominant axis, say $\lambda_1=1000$ and the other $999$ eigenvalues $1$:

$$\text{random}=\frac{1000+999\cdot1}{1000}\approx2, \qquad \text{PC1}=1000, \qquad \text{ratio}\approx500.$$

Derive it yourself

Prove $\mathbb{E}_u[u^TCu]=\operatorname{tr}C/d$ for $u$ uniform on the unit sphere.

Solution

A scalar is its own trace, and $\operatorname{tr}(AB)=\operatorname{tr}(BA)$; then $\mathbb{E}$ is linear and passes through $\operatorname{tr}$ and the constant $C$:

$$u^TCu=\operatorname{tr}(u^TCu)=\operatorname{tr}(Cuu^T) \ \Rightarrow\ \mathbb{E}[u^TCu]=\operatorname{tr}\big(C\,\mathbb{E}[uu^T]\big).$$

Now find $\mathbb{E}[uu^T]$ by symmetry. Flipping the sign of coordinate $i$ maps the sphere to itself, so $u$ and the flipped $u$ have the same distribution:

$$\mathbb{E}[u_iu_j]=\mathbb{E}[(-u_i)u_j]=-\mathbb{E}[u_iu_j]\ \Rightarrow\ \mathbb{E}[u_iu_j]=0 \quad(i\ne j).$$

Swapping coordinates $i,j$ also preserves the distribution, so all $\mathbb{E}[u_i^2]$ are equal; and they add to $\mathbb{E}\|u\|^2=1$:

$$d\,\mathbb{E}[u_1^2]=1 \ \Rightarrow\ \mathbb{E}[uu^T]=\frac Id \ \Rightarrow\ \mathbb{E}[u^TCu]=\operatorname{tr}\Big(\frac Cd\Big)=\frac{\operatorname{tr}C}d.$$
