Gradient descent on strongly convex functions

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran
optimizationgradient-descentconvergenceproofscondition-number

Builds on: Gradient descent on smooth convex functions, Smoothness and strong convexity, Gradient descent on a quadratic · Leads to: Momentum and Nesterov acceleration

Add a floor on the curvature ($\mu\gt0$) and the $1/k$ rate becomes geometric: every step removes at least the fraction $1/\kappa$ of the remaining gap. The proof is two inequalities, one line each.

The claim

$f$ is $\mu$-strongly convex and $L$-smooth, $\kappa=L/\mu$, step size $1/L$.

$$x_{k+1}=x_k-\tfrac1L\nabla f(x_k) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(x_k)-f^*\le\Big(1-\frac1\kappa\Big)^k\big(f(x_0)-f^*\big).$$

Shorthand as before: $g_k=\nabla f(x_k)$, $\ \text{gap}_k=f(x_k)-f^*$.

The proof

The descent lemma says a step removes $\|g\|^2/2L$. PL says $\|g\|^2$ is at least $2\mu$ times the gap. Chain them.

Step 1: descent lemma (previous note), minus $f^*$ on both sides:

$$\text{gap}_{k+1}\le\text{gap}_k-\frac{\|g_k\|^2}{2L}.$$

Step 2: PL, $\|g_k\|^2\ge2\mu\,\text{gap}_k$, makes the subtracted term at least $\tfrac\mu L\text{gap}_k$:

$$\text{gap}_{k+1}\le\text{gap}_k-\frac{2\mu\,\text{gap}_k}{2L}=\Big(1-\frac\mu L\Big)\text{gap}_k.$$

Step 3: induction. True at $k=0$. If $\text{gap}_k\le(1-\tfrac\mu L)^k\text{gap}_0$, then by Step 2

$$\text{gap}_{k+1}\le\Big(1-\frac\mu L\Big)\text{gap}_k\le\Big(1-\frac\mu L\Big)^{k+1}\text{gap}_0. \qquad\blacksquare$$

Strong convexity entered only through PL, so any $L$-smooth $f$ satisfying PL gets the same rate, convex or not.

How many iterations

A linear rate with $\rho=1-1/\kappa$: the steps grow in proportion to $\kappa$, and only logarithmically in the accuracy.

From convergence rates, $\ln(1/\rho)\ge1/\kappa$, so

$$k\ \ge\ \kappa\,\ln\frac{\text{gap}_0}{\varepsilon} \quad\text{steps suffice.}$$

Compare with the exact quadratic answer

On a quadratic we know the exact per-direction factors $1-\eta\lambda_i$. With $\eta=1/L$ they give $(1-1/\kappa)^2$ per step on $f$, so the general proof is valid but loses a square.

From gradient descent on a quadratic, $x-x^*=\sum_ic_iq_i$ with $c_i\leftarrow(1-\eta\lambda_i)c_i$. With $x^*=0$ and orthonormal $q_i$:

$$\text{gap}=\tfrac12x^TAx=\tfrac12\sum_i\lambda_ic_i^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{each term is multiplied by } (1-\eta\lambda_i)^2.$$

With $\eta=\tfrac1L$ and $\lambda_i\in[\mu,L]$, each factor $1-\lambda_i/L\in[0,\,1-\tfrac1\kappa]$, so each step multiplies $\text{gap}$ by at most $(1-\tfrac1\kappa)^2\le1-\tfrac1\kappa$.

Where the square goes. One direction, curvature $\lambda$, $t=\lambda/L$: $\text{gap}=\tfrac12\lambda c^2$ and $g=\lambda c$. The descent lemma promises a drop of

$$\frac{g^2}{2L}=\frac{\lambda^2c^2}{2L}=t\cdot\text{gap},$$

but the actual new gap is $(1-t)^2\,\text{gap}$, so the actual drop is

$$\big(1-(1-t)^2\big)\text{gap}=(2t-t^2)\,\text{gap}\ \ge\ t\cdot\text{gap} \qquad (t\le1).$$

The lemma charges the step for curvature $L$ when the true curvature is $\lambda$, so it under-promises.

Bonus: the iterates contract too

For twice continuously differentiable $f$, one step is "multiply the error by an average of $I-H/L$", and every such matrix shrinks vectors by at least $1-1/\kappa$.

Step 1. $\nabla f(x^*)=0$, so with $e=x_k-x^*$:

$$x_{k+1}-x^*=e-\tfrac1L\big(\nabla f(x_k)-\nabla f(x^*)\big).$$

Step 2. Fundamental theorem of calculus on the gradient along the segment; by the chain rule $\tfrac{d}{dt}\nabla f(x^*+te)=H_te$ with $H_t=H(x^*+te)$:

$$\nabla f(x_k)-\nabla f(x^*)=\int_0^1H_te\,dt,$$

$$x_{k+1}-x^*=\int_0^1\big(I-\tfrac1LH_t\big)e\,dt.$$

Step 3. $I-H_t/L$ is symmetric with eigenvalues $s_i=1-\lambda_i/L\in[0,1-\tfrac1\kappa]$. In its eigenbasis, $\|(I-H_t/L)v\|^2=\sum_is_i^2c_i^2\le(1-\tfrac1\kappa)^2\|v\|^2$. The norm of an integral is at most the integral of the norm:

$$\|x_{k+1}-x^*\|\le\int_0^1\big\|(I-\tfrac1LH_t)e\big\|\,dt\le\Big(1-\frac1\kappa\Big)\|x_k-x^*\|. \qquad\blacksquare$$

Check: $\|x_1\|=\tfrac{\sqrt2}3=0.471\le\tfrac23\|x_0\|=0.667$, and from then on the factor is exactly $\tfrac23$: tight here.

Derive it yourself

With $\kappa=100$, how many iterations of gradient descent with step $1/L$ does the bound need to reduce $f-f^*$ by a factor $10^{-6}$? Compare with $\kappa=3$, and with the exact quadratic factor $(1-1/\kappa)^2$.

Solution

$\rho=1-\tfrac1{100}=0.99$. Need $0.99^k\le10^{-6}$; take logs, divide by $\ln0.99\lt0$ (flip), with $\ln10^6=13.82$:

$$k\ge\frac{13.82}{\ln(1/0.99)}=\frac{13.82}{0.01005}=1374.6\ \Rightarrow\ 1375.$$

The shortcut $\kappa\ln10^6=100\times13.82=1382$ agrees. About $2.303/0.01005=229$ steps per digit.

$\kappa=3$: $13.82/\ln1.5=13.82/0.405=34.1\Rightarrow35$ steps. On a quadratic with $\kappa=100$ the true factor is $0.99^2$, so $\ln(1/0.99^2)=0.0201$ and $13.82/0.0201=687.3\Rightarrow688$ steps, half the bound.
