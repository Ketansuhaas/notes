Dynamics in the eigenbasis

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran
linear-algebraeigenvalueseigenbasisdynamical-systems

Builds on: Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and defectiveness, The spectral theorem · Leads to: Quadratic forms and positive definiteness, Gradient descent on a quadratic

Applying $A$ over and over is hard to picture. In the eigenbasis it splits into $n$ independent 1-D problems: each coordinate just gets multiplied by its own $\lambda_i$.

Coordinates in the eigenbasis

The eigenvectors form a new coordinate system; $c$ holds $x_0$'s coordinates in it.

$$\underbrace{x_0}_{n\times1}=c_1v_1+\cdots+c_nv_n=\underbrace{V}_{n\times n}\underbrace{c}_{n\times1} \iff c=V^{-1}x_0.$$

Example

$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, $v_1=(1,1)$ with $\lambda_1=3$, $v_2=(1,-1)$ with $\lambda_2=1$ (from the eigenvalue note). Solve $Vc=x_0$ for $x_0=(4,0)$:

$$c_1\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}+c_2\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\0\end{pmatrix}: \quad c_1+c_2=4,\ \ c_1-c_2=0 \ \Rightarrow\ c_1=c_2=2.$$

So $x_0=2v_1+2v_2$.

Repeated multiplication

Each eigen-direction grows or shrinks on its own, by a factor $\lambda_i$ per step.

Each application of $A$ multiplies an eigenvector by $\lambda_i$ once more:

$$A^kv_i=A^{k-1}(Av_i)=\lambda_iA^{k-1}v_i=\cdots=\lambda_i^kv_i.$$

$A^k$ is linear, so apply it term by term to $x_0=\sum_ic_iv_i$:

$$A^kx_0=\sum_ic_i\lambda_i^kv_i=\underbrace{V}_{n\times n}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1^k&&\\&\ddots&\\&&\lambda_n^k\end{pmatrix}}_{\Lambda^k}\underbrace{V^{-1}x_0}_{c}.$$

The matrix form also follows from $A=V\Lambda V^{-1}$: neighbouring $V^{-1}V$ pairs cancel,

$$A^k=V\Lambda\underbrace{V^{-1}V}_{I}\Lambda V^{-1}\cdots=V\Lambda^kV^{-1},$$

and a power of a diagonal matrix just raises each diagonal entry to that power.

Example

$$A^kx_0=2\cdot3^k\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}+2\cdot1^k\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2\cdot3^k+2\\2\cdot3^k-2\end{pmatrix}.$$

$k=1,2,3$: $(6+2,\,6-2)=(8,4)$, $(18+2,\,18-2)=(20,16)$, $(54+2,\,54-2)=(56,52)$. Direct multiplication agrees:

$$A\begin{pmatrix}4\\0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}8\\4\end{pmatrix},\quad A\begin{pmatrix}8\\4\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}16+4\\8+8\end{pmatrix},\quad A\begin{pmatrix}20\\16\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}40+16\\20+32\end{pmatrix}. \ \checkmark$$

The dominant eigenvalue wins

The largest $|\lambda|$ outgrows the rest, so repeated multiplication turns almost any vector toward the top eigenvector. That is power iteration.

Factor $\lambda_1^k$ out of the sum (order $|\lambda_1|\gt|\lambda_2|\ge\cdots$, and assume $c_1\neq0$):

$$A^kx_0=\lambda_1^k\Big(c_1v_1+\sum_{i\ge2}c_i\big(\tfrac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\big)^kv_i\Big).$$

Every $|\lambda_i/\lambda_1|\lt1$, so $(\lambda_i/\lambda_1)^k\to0$ and

$$A^kx_0\ \approx\ c_1\lambda_1^kv_1.$$

The rate. Measure how much of $v_i$ is left relative to $v_1$:

$$\frac{\text{coefficient of } v_i}{\text{coefficient of } v_1}=\frac{c_i\lambda_i^k}{c_1\lambda_1^k}=\frac{c_i}{c_1}\Big(\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\Big)^k.$$

Each step multiplies it by $\lambda_i/\lambda_1$. The slowest to die is $i=2$, so the error shrinks like $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|^k$.

Example

Second/first component of $A^kx_0$, from the formula above:

$$\frac{2\cdot3^k-2}{2\cdot3^k+2}=\frac{3^k-1}{3^k+1}: \quad k=1:\tfrac24=0.5,\ \ k=2:\tfrac8{10}=0.8,\ \ k=3:\tfrac{26}{28}\approx0.929,$$

$\to1$, the slope of $v_1=(1,1)$. The gap to $1$ is

$$1-\frac{3^k-1}{3^k+1}=\frac{2}{3^k+1}\approx2\cdot\big(\tfrac13\big)^k: \quad 0.5,\ 0.2,\ 0.071,$$

shrinking by about $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|=\tfrac13$ per step (ratios $0.4$, $0.36$, heading to $0.33$).

Continuous time

Same story for $\dot x=Ax$. Each coordinate solves $\dot y=\lambda y$, so powers $\lambda^k$ become exponentials $e^{\lambda t}$.

Step 1: switch to eigen-coordinates. Write $x(t)=Vy(t)$. $V$ is constant, so $\dot x=V\dot y$, and

$$V\dot y=AVy=V\Lambda y \qquad (AV=V\Lambda)$$

$$\Rightarrow\ \dot y=\Lambda y \iff \dot y_i=\lambda_iy_i \ \text{ for each } i \qquad (\text{multiply by } V^{-1}).$$

Step 2: solve one coordinate. $\tfrac{d}{dt}e^{\lambda t}=\lambda e^{\lambda t}$, so $y_i=c_ie^{\lambda_it}$ works. It is the only solution: for any solution,

$$\tfrac{d}{dt}\big(e^{-\lambda_it}y_i\big)=e^{-\lambda_it}(\dot y_i-\lambda_iy_i)=0,$$

so $e^{-\lambda_it}y_i$ stays at its $t=0$ value $y_i(0)=c_i$, where $c=y(0)=V^{-1}x_0$.

Step 3: switch back. $x=Vy$:

$$x(t)=\sum_ic_ie^{\lambda_it}v_i=V\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}e^{\lambda_1t}&&\\&\ddots&\\&&e^{\lambda_nt}\end{pmatrix}}_{e^{\Lambda t}}V^{-1}x_0.$$

Growth, decay, spin. Size depends only on $|\lambda|$ for steps and on $\operatorname{Re}\lambda$ for flow. Write $\lambda=re^{i\theta}=a+ib$:

$$\lambda^k=r^ke^{ik\theta} \ \Rightarrow\ |\lambda^k|=r^k=|\lambda|^k, \ \text{turning by } \theta \text{ each step};$$

$$e^{\lambda t}=e^{at}\,(\cos bt+i\sin bt) \ \Rightarrow\ |e^{\lambda t}|=e^{at}=e^{(\operatorname{Re}\lambda)t}, \ \text{turning at rate } b.$$

Example

Same $c=(2,2)$ and $\lambda=3,1$ as before:

$$x(t)=2e^{3t}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}+2e^{t}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad x(0)=\begin{pmatrix}2+2\\2-2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}4\\0\end{pmatrix}. \ \checkmark$$

The ratio of the two parts is $\tfrac{2e^{t}}{2e^{3t}}=e^{-2t}\to0$, so the $e^{3t}$ term swamps the other and $x(t)$ bends toward $v_1$.

Why this matters

Power iteration, PageRankrepeat $A$, read off the top eigenvector
Markov chain $x_{k+1}=Px_k$dominant $\lambda=1$: converges to the stationary distribution
Deep networkslayers act like $A^k$: $|\lambda|$ (or singular values) far from $1$ means exploding or vanishing signals
Gradient descent$x_{k+1}=(I-\eta A)x_k$: factor $1-\eta\lambda_i$ per direction

The gradient descent row. On $f(x)=\tfrac12x^TAx$ with $A$ symmetric, $\nabla f=Ax$, so one step is

$$x_{k+1}=x_k-\eta Ax_k=(I-\eta A)x_k, \qquad (I-\eta A)v_i=v_i-\eta\lambda_iv_i=(1-\eta\lambda_i)v_i.$$

Same eigenvectors, eigenvalues $1-\eta\lambda_i$: everything above applies with $\lambda_i$ replaced by $1-\eta\lambda_i$. See gradient descent on a quadratic.

The Markov row. Columns of $P$ are probability vectors, so they sum to $1$: $\mathbf 1^TP=\mathbf 1^T$. So $1$ is an eigenvalue of $P^T$, hence of $P$ (same $\det(P-\lambda I)$, since $\det M^T=\det M$). No eigenvalue exceeds $1$ in size: entries are $\ge0$, so

$$\textstyle\sum_i|(Px)_i|\le\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}|x_j|=\sum_j|x_j|,$$

and $Pv=\lambda v$ then gives $|\lambda|\sum|v_j|\le\sum|v_j|$. Proof idea for convergence: if every other $|\lambda|\lt1$ (true when some power of $P$ has all entries positive, by the Perron-Frobenius theorem), the dominant-eigenvalue argument leaves only the $\lambda=1$ eigenvector.

Derive it yourself

For diagonalizable $A$, prove: $A^kx_0\to0$ for every $x_0$ $\iff$ every $|\lambda_i|\lt1$.

Solution

($\Leftarrow$) Write $x_0=\sum_ic_iv_i$. The triangle inequality bounds the size:

$$\|A^kx_0\|=\Big\|\sum_ic_i\lambda_i^kv_i\Big\|\le\sum_i|c_i|\,|\lambda_i|^k\,\|v_i\|\to0,$$

since each $|\lambda_i|^k\to0$ when $|\lambda_i|\lt1$, and there are finitely many terms.

($\Rightarrow$) If some $|\lambda_j|\ge1$, start at $x_0=v_j$:

$$\|A^kv_j\|=\|\lambda_j^kv_j\|=|\lambda_j|^k\|v_j\|\ge\|v_j\|\gt0,$$

which never reaches $0$.

Step-size rule. For $I-\eta A$ with $A$ symmetric and all $\lambda_i\gt0$, the condition is $|1-\eta\lambda_i|\lt1$ for every $i$:

$$-1\lt1-\eta\lambda_i\lt1 \iff 0\lt\eta\lambda_i\lt2 \iff 0\lt\eta\lt\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}.$$
