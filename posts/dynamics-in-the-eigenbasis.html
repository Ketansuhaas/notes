Dynamics in the eigenbasis

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran
linear-algebraeigenvalueseigenbasisdynamical-systems

Builds on: Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and defectiveness, The spectral theorem · Leads to: Quadratic forms and positive definiteness, Gradient descent on a quadratic

Applying $A$ over and over is hard to picture. In the eigenbasis it splits into $n$ independent 1-D problems: each coordinate just gets multiplied by its own $\lambda_i$.

Coordinates in the eigenbasis

The eigenvectors form a new coordinate system; $c$ holds $x_0$'s coordinates in it.

$$\underbrace{x_0}_{n\times1}=c_1v_1+\cdots+c_nv_n=\underbrace{V}_{n\times n}\underbrace{c}_{n\times1} \iff c=V^{-1}x_0.$$

Example

$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$, $v_1=(1,1)$, $v_2=(1,-1)$: $\ x_0=\begin{pmatrix}4\\0\end{pmatrix}=2v_1+2v_2$.

Repeated multiplication

Each eigen-direction grows or shrinks on its own, by a factor $\lambda_i$ per step.

$$A^kx_0=\sum_ic_i\lambda_i^kv_i=\underbrace{V}_{n\times n}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1^k&&\\&\ddots&\\&&\lambda_n^k\end{pmatrix}}_{\Lambda^k}\underbrace{V^{-1}x_0}_{c}.$$

Example

$A^kx_0=2\cdot3^k(1,1)+2(1,-1)=(2\cdot3^k+2,\ 2\cdot3^k-2)$. For $k=1,2,3$: $(8,4),\ (20,16),\ (56,52)$, matching direct multiplication. ✓

The dominant eigenvalue wins

The largest $|\lambda|$ outgrows the rest, so repeated multiplication turns almost any vector toward the top eigenvector. That is power iteration.

$$A^kx_0=\lambda_1^k\Big(c_1v_1+\sum_{i\ge2}c_i\big(\tfrac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_1}\big)^kv_i\Big)\ \approx\ c_1\lambda_1^kv_1 \qquad(|\lambda_1|\gt|\lambda_i|,\ c_1\neq0).$$

The ratio $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|$ sets how fast the other terms die.

Example

Second/first component: $0.5,\ 0.8,\ 0.929,\dots\to1$, so the direction approaches $v_1=(1,1)$, with the gap shrinking by about $|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|=\tfrac13$ per step.

Continuous time

Same story for $\dot x=Ax$. Each coordinate solves $\dot y=\lambda y$, so powers $\lambda^k$ become exponentials $e^{\lambda t}$.

$$x(t)=\sum_ic_ie^{\lambda_it}v_i=V\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}e^{\lambda_1t}&&\\&\ddots&\\&&e^{\lambda_nt}\end{pmatrix}}_{e^{\Lambda t}}V^{-1}x_0.$$

Example

$x(t)=2e^{3t}(1,1)+2e^{t}(1,-1)$: the $e^{3t}$ term swamps the other, so $x(t)$ bends toward $v_1$.

Why this matters

Power iteration, PageRankrepeat $A$, read off the top eigenvector
Markov chain $x_{k+1}=Px_k$dominant $\lambda=1$: converges to the stationary distribution
Deep networkslayers act like $A^k$: $|\lambda|$ (or singular values) far from $1$ means exploding or vanishing signals
Gradient descent$x_{k+1}=(I-\eta A)x_k$: factor $1-\eta\lambda_i$ per direction

Derive it yourself

For diagonalizable $A$, prove: $A^kx_0\to0$ for every $x_0$ $\iff$ every $|\lambda_i|\lt1$.

Solution

($\Leftarrow$) $A^kx_0=\sum_ic_i\lambda_i^kv_i$ and each $\lambda_i^k\to0$. ($\Rightarrow$) If some $|\lambda_j|\ge1$, start at $x_0=v_j$: $\|A^kv_j\|=|\lambda_j|^k\|v_j\|\ge\|v_j\|\gt0$, which never reaches $0$. Applied to $I-\eta A$, this is the step-size rule for gradient descent.
