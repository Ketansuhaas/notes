Quadratic forms and positive definiteness

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran

Builds on: Four views of matrix multiplication, The spectral theorem · Leads to: Singular value decomposition, Taylor expansion in many variables, The covariance matrix, intuitively

$x^TAx$ is the simplest curved function: a bowl, a dome, or a saddle. The signs of $A$'s eigenvalues tell you which.

A matrix squeezed into a number

Sandwich $A$ between a row and a column and every $x$ gets one number. Plot it and you get a curved surface.

$$f(x)=\underbrace{x^T}_{1\times n}\underbrace{A}_{n\times n}\underbrace{x}_{n\times1}=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}\,x_ix_j,$$ $$A=A^T \ \ (\text{only the symmetric part of } A \text{ contributes}).$$

Example

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}: \qquad f(x)=2x_1^2+2x_1x_2+2x_2^2, \qquad f(1,1)=6.$$

In the eigenbasis: a sum of squares

Rotate onto the eigenvector axes and the cross terms vanish. Each axis adds its eigenvalue times a square.

$$A=Q\Lambda Q^T, \quad \underbrace{y}_{n\times1}=\underbrace{Q^T}_{n\times n}\underbrace{x}_{n\times1}: \qquad f(x)=y^T\Lambda y=\sum_i\lambda_iy_i^2.$$

Example

$\lambda_1=3$ along $q_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, $\lambda_2=1$ along $q_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$. For $x=(1,1)$: $y=(\sqrt2,0)$, so $f=3\cdot2+1\cdot0=6$. ✓

Bowl, dome, or saddle

Each $\lambda_i$ is the curvature along its axis. All curving up: a bowl with one bottom. All down: a dome. Mixed: a saddle.

Why: $f(q_i)=\lambda_i$, and $\sum_i\lambda_iy_i^2\gt0$ for every $y\ne0$ iff every $\lambda_i\gt0$. A $2\times2$ test with no eigenvalues:

$$\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{pmatrix}\succ0 \iff a\gt0 \ \text{ and } \ ad-b^2\gt0.$$

Example

$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$$a=2$, $ad-b^2=3$$\lambda=3,1$bowl
$B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$$a=0$ fails$\lambda=1,-1$saddle: $x^TBx=2x_1x_2$

Up close, near a point where the slope is zero, every smooth function looks like a quadratic form. So bowl, dome, or saddle is exactly how minima, maxima, and saddle points are classified; see Taylor expansion.

Derive it yourself

Show $M^TM\succeq0$ for any matrix $M$ (any shape), and $M^TM\succ0$ exactly when $M$ has independent columns.

Solution

$x^TM^TMx=(Mx)^T(Mx)=\|Mx\|^2\ge0$. It equals $0$ iff $Mx=0$, and independent columns mean $Mx=0$ only for $x=0$. This is why $A^TA$ in the SVD and every covariance matrix are positive semidefinite.
