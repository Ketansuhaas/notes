The spectral theorem

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran
linear-algebraeigenvaluesdiagonalizationsymmetric-matrices

Builds on: Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and defectiveness, Rank, subspaces, and projections · Leads to: Dynamics in the eigenbasis, Quadratic forms and positive definiteness, The covariance matrix, intuitively

Symmetric matrices are the best case: rotate into perpendicular axes, stretch along each, rotate back. Covariances, Hessians, and kernels are all symmetric.

Statement

If $A=A^T$, its eigenvalues are real and its eigenvectors can be chosen perpendicular and unit length.

$$ \underbrace{A}_{n\times n}= \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\vert&&\vert\\q_1&\cdots&q_n\\\vert&&\vert\end{pmatrix}}_{Q} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&&\\&\ddots&\\&&\lambda_n\end{pmatrix}}_{\Lambda} \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}-\,q_1^T-\\\vdots\\-\,q_n^T-\end{pmatrix}}_{Q^T}, \qquad Q^TQ=I\ \ (Q^{-1}=Q^T). $$

$Q^TQ=I$ is just orthonormality written out: entry $(i,j)$ of $Q^TQ$ is $\text{row}_i(Q^T)\cdot\text{col}_j(Q)=q_i^Tq_j$, which is $1$ if $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise. Then $AQ=Q\Lambda$ (column $i$: $Aq_i=\lambda_iq_i$), times $Q^{-1}=Q^T$ on the right, gives the boxed form. The sections below derive each claim.

Example

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ come from the eigenvalue note: $3$ along $(1,1)$, $1$ along $(1,-1)$. Divide each by its length $\sqrt2$:

$$q_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix},\ q_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix},\ \Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

$$Q\Lambda=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\3&-1\end{pmatrix} \qquad (\text{column } i \text{ scaled by } \lambda_i),$$

$$Q\Lambda Q^T=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\3&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}3+1&3-1\\3-1&3+1\end{pmatrix}=A. \ \checkmark$$

Eigenvalues are real

Complex eigenvalues signal rotation. Symmetry leaves no room for it.

Allow complex $\lambda,v$ with $Av=\lambda v$. Write $v^H=\bar v^T$ (conjugate transpose). $A$ is real and symmetric, so $A^H=\bar A^T=A$. Compute $v^HAv$ two ways:

$$v^H(Av)=v^H(\lambda v)=\lambda\,v^Hv,$$

$$v^HAv=v^HA^Hv=(Av)^Hv=(\lambda v)^Hv=\bar\lambda\,v^Hv.$$

$v^Hv=\sum_i|v_i|^2\gt0$ since $v\neq0$, so divide: $\lambda=\bar\lambda$, i.e. $\lambda$ is real. Then $A-\lambda I$ is a real matrix, so its null space has a real nonzero vector: eigenvectors can be taken real.

Example

$\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ (rotation, not symmetric)$\det=\lambda^2+1=0$$\lambda=\pm i$
$\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ (symmetric)$\det=(2-\lambda)^2-1=0$$\lambda=3,1$

Eigenvectors are orthogonal

Symmetry lets $\lambda$ slide across the dot product. That forces eigenvectors of different eigenvalues to be perpendicular.

Take $Av_1=\lambda_1v_1$, $Av_2=\lambda_2v_2$, and move $A$ from one side of the dot product to the other:

$$\lambda_1v_1^Tv_2=(Av_1)^Tv_2 \qquad (Av_1=\lambda_1v_1)$$

$$=v_1^TA^Tv_2=v_1^TAv_2 \qquad (A^T=A)$$

$$=\lambda_2v_1^Tv_2 \qquad (Av_2=\lambda_2v_2).$$

Subtract: $(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\,v_1^Tv_2=0$, and $\lambda_1\neq\lambda_2$, so $v_1^Tv_2=0$.

Example

$q_1\cdot q_2=\tfrac12(1-1)=0$. ✓

What if an eigenvalue repeats?

A repeated $\lambda$ gives a whole eigenspace of full dimension; pick any orthonormal basis inside it. More freedom, never fewer eigenvectors.

$$A=A^T \ \Rightarrow\ A=Q\Lambda Q^T \text{ always} \ \Rightarrow\ \operatorname{GM}(\lambda)=\operatorname{AM}(\lambda)\ \ \forall\lambda\ \text{(never defective)}.$$

The orthogonality argument above says nothing when $\lambda_1=\lambda_2$, so we build $Q$ directly, one axis at a time.

Step 1: peel off one axis. Take a real unit eigenvector $q_1$, $Aq_1=\lambda_1q_1$ (one exists: $\det(A-\lambda I)$ has a root, possibly complex, and the previous section makes it real). Complete it to an orthonormal basis; call the other columns $W$ ($n\times(n-1)$, $W^Tq_1=0$).

Step 2: $A$ keeps the rest perpendicular to $q_1$. For $w\perp q_1$:

$$q_1^T(Aw)=(Aq_1)^Tw=\lambda_1\,q_1^Tw=0 \qquad (A^T=A).$$

So in the basis $(q_1\ W)$, the first row and column are zero apart from the corner:

$$\begin{pmatrix}q_1^T\\W^T\end{pmatrix}A\begin{pmatrix}q_1&W\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}q_1^TAq_1&q_1^TAW\\W^TAq_1&W^TAW\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&0\\0&A_1\end{pmatrix},$$

with $q_1^TAq_1=\lambda_1q_1^Tq_1=\lambda_1$, $W^TAq_1=\lambda_1W^Tq_1=0$, $q_1^TAW=0$ by Step 2.

Step 3: repeat. $A_1=W^TAW$ is $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ and symmetric, since $(W^TAW)^T=W^TA^TW=W^TAW$. By induction on size (a $1\times1$ matrix is already diagonal), $A_1=Q_1\Lambda_1Q_1^T$. Stack the axes:

$$Q=\begin{pmatrix}q_1&WQ_1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q^TAQ=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&0\\0&Q_1^TA_1Q_1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_1&0\\0&\Lambda_1\end{pmatrix}.$$

$Q$ has orthonormal columns ($(WQ_1)^T(WQ_1)=Q_1^TW^TWQ_1=I$, and $W^Tq_1=0$), so $A=Q\Lambda Q^T$. This holds with repeats too, giving $n$ independent eigenvectors, so $\operatorname{GM}=\operatorname{AM}$ for every $\lambda$.

Never defective is not full rank: eigenvalues can still be $0$.

Example: all-ones $3\times3$

$A=\mathbf 1\mathbf 1^T$ with $\mathbf 1=(1,1,1)$, so $Ax=\mathbf 1(\mathbf 1^Tx)=(x_1+x_2+x_3)\,\mathbf 1$:

$$A\mathbf 1=3\,\mathbf 1\ \ (\lambda=3), \qquad Ax=0 \iff x_1+x_2+x_3=0\ \ (\lambda=0).$$

That plane has $\dim 2$ (two free coordinates), so $\operatorname{GM}(0)=2$; with $3$ it accounts for all $n=3$ eigenvalues: $3,0,0$, and $\operatorname{AM}(0)=2=\operatorname{GM}(0)$. Yet $\operatorname{rank}A=3-2=1$. Pick any orthonormal pair in the plane:

$$q_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(1,1,1),\quad q_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1,0),\quad q_3=\tfrac1{\sqrt6}(1,1,-2).$$

Check: entries of $q_2$ and $q_3$ sum to $0$ (in the plane), and $q_2\cdot q_3=\tfrac{1-1+0}{\sqrt{12}}=0$. ✓

$A$ as a sum of rank-1 pieces

Project onto each axis, scale by $\lambda_i$, add up. This is view 4 of matrix multiplication applied to $Q\Lambda Q^T$.

Group $Q\Lambda Q^T$ as $(Q\Lambda)Q^T$. Column $i$ of $Q\Lambda$ is $\lambda_iq_i$ and row $i$ of $Q^T$ is $q_i^T$, so view 4 gives

$$A=\sum_i\lambda_i\underbrace{q_i}_{n\times1}\underbrace{q_i^T}_{1\times n}.$$

Each $q_iq_i^T$ is the projection onto the line through $q_i$: the line formula $\dfrac{aa^T}{a^Ta}$ from projections with $a=q_i$, $q_i^Tq_i=1$. Applied to $x$: $q_iq_i^Tx=(q_i^Tx)\,q_i$, the $q_i$-coordinate of $x$ times $q_i$.

Example

$$q_1q_1^T=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad q_2q_2^T=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix},$$

$$3\cdot\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}+1\cdot\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}3+1&3-1\\3-1&3+1\end{pmatrix}=A. \ \checkmark$$

Derive it yourself

If every $\lambda_i\neq0$, show $A^{-1}=\sum_i\frac1{\lambda_i}q_iq_i^T$. Check it on the running example.

Solution

Multiply out the two sums (distribute every term against every term):

$$\Big(\sum_i\lambda_iq_iq_i^T\Big)\Big(\sum_j\tfrac1{\lambda_j}q_jq_j^T\Big)=\sum_{i,j}\tfrac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_j}\,q_i\underbrace{(q_i^Tq_j)}_{1 \text{ if } i=j,\ \text{else } 0}q_j^T=\sum_iq_iq_i^T.$$

By view 4, $\sum_iq_iq_i^T=QQ^T$. $Q$ is square with $Q^TQ=I$, so $Q^T=Q^{-1}$ and also $QQ^T=I$. So the second sum is $A^{-1}$.

Example: $\lambda_1=3$, $\lambda_2=1$:

$$\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}+\tfrac11\cdot\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac16+\tfrac12&\tfrac16-\tfrac12\\\tfrac16-\tfrac12&\tfrac16+\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix},$$

$$\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\cdot\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}=\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}4-1&-2+2\\2-2&-1+4\end{pmatrix}=I. \ \checkmark$$
