Singular value decomposition

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran
linear-algebraeigenvaluessvdmatrix-factorization

Builds on: Rank, subspaces, and projections, The spectral theorem, Quadratic forms and positive definiteness · Leads to: Principal component analysis

Every matrix, any shape, is a rotation, then a stretch along the axes, then another rotation.

The statement

Pick the right orthonormal axes in the input and in the output, and $A$ just scales axis $i$ by $\sigma_i$.

$$\underbrace{A}_{m\times n}=\underbrace{U}_{m\times m}\,\underbrace{\Sigma}_{m\times n}\,\underbrace{V^T}_{n\times n}, \qquad Av_i=\sigma_iu_i, \qquad \sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\cdots\ge0.$$

$U,V$ orthogonal. $\Sigma$ is diagonal, padded with zero rows (tall $A$) or zero columns (wide $A$). Only $r=\operatorname{rank}A$ terms survive (thin SVD):

$$\underbrace{A}_{m\times n}=\underbrace{U_r}_{m\times r}\,\underbrace{\Sigma_r}_{r\times r}\,\underbrace{V_r^T}_{r\times n}.$$

Constructing it

$A$ may be rectangular, but $A^TA$ and $AA^T$ are square, symmetric, and positive semidefinite. The spectral theorem always applies to them, so every matrix has an SVD.

$$\underbrace{A^TA}_{n\times n}=V(\Sigma^T\Sigma)V^T, \qquad \underbrace{AA^T}_{m\times m}=U(\Sigma\Sigma^T)U^T,$$ $$\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}, \quad u_i=\frac{Av_i}{\sigma_i},\ \ v_i=\frac{A^Tu_i}{\sigma_i}\ \ (\sigma_i\gt0).$$

Example

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad AA^T=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix} \ \Rightarrow\ \sigma_1=\sqrt3,\ \sigma_2=1.$$

$$u_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1),\ \ v_1=\tfrac{A^Tu_1}{\sqrt3}=\tfrac1{\sqrt6}(1,1,2); \qquad u_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1),\ \ v_2=A^Tu_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1,0).$$

Check: $Av_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt6}(3,3)=\sqrt3\,u_1$. ✓

Four subspaces for free

The columns of $U$ and $V$ split into orthonormal bases for all four fundamental subspaces at once.

first $r$ columnsremaining columns
$U$ (output, $\mathbb{R}^m$)$C(A)$$N(A^T)$
$V$ (input, $\mathbb{R}^n$)$C(A^T)$$N(A)$

$$\operatorname{rank}A=\text{number of nonzero } \sigma_i.$$

Example

$r=2=m$: $C(A)=\mathbb{R}^2$, $N(A^T)=\{0\}$. The leftover $v_3=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(-1,-1,1)$ spans $N(A)$: $Av_3=0$. ✓

Low-rank approximation

The SVD writes $A$ as a sum of rank-one pieces, biggest first. Keep the top $k$ and you have the best rank-$k$ copy of $A$: the idea behind compression and PCA.

$$\underbrace{A}_{m\times n}=\sum_{i=1}^r\sigma_i\,\underbrace{u_i}_{m\times1}\underbrace{v_i^T}_{1\times n}, \qquad A_k=\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^T, \qquad \|A-A_k\|_F^2=\sum_{i\gt k}\sigma_i^2.$$

No rank-$k$ matrix gets closer (Eckart-Young).

Example

$$A_1=\sqrt3\,u_1v_1^T=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1&2\\1&1&2\end{pmatrix},$$ $$A-A_1=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\-1&1&0\end{pmatrix}=\sigma_2u_2v_2^T, \qquad \|A-A_1\|_F^2=1=\sigma_2^2. \ \checkmark$$

The pseudoinverse

Invert the stretches you can, zero out the ones you can't. The result solves $Ax=b$ as well as possible, with the shortest $x$.

$$\underbrace{A^+}_{n\times m}=\underbrace{V}_{n\times n}\,\underbrace{\Sigma^+}_{n\times m}\,\underbrace{U^T}_{m\times m}, \qquad \Sigma^+_{ii}=\begin{cases}1/\sigma_i & \sigma_i\gt0\\0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}, \qquad x^+=A^+b.$$

$x^+$ is the minimum-norm least-squares solution; it lies in the row space $C(A^T)$.

Example

$b=(1,1)=\sqrt2\,u_1$, so $x^+=\tfrac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt3}v_1=\tfrac13(1,1,2)$. $Ax^+=(1,1)$ ✓, and $x^+\perp v_3$: adding any null-space piece only makes $x$ longer.

Derive it yourself

Show $\max_{\|x\|=1}\|Ax\|=\sigma_1$, reached at $x=v_1$. (The largest stretch of $A$ is its top singular value.)

Solution

Write $x=\sum_ic_iv_i$ with $\sum_ic_i^2=1$ ($V$ is an orthonormal basis; set $\sigma_i=0$ for $i\gt r$). Then $\|Ax\|^2=x^TA^TAx=\sum_i\sigma_i^2c_i^2\le\sigma_1^2\sum_ic_i^2=\sigma_1^2$, with equality at $c=(1,0,\dots)$, i.e. $x=v_1$. Example: $\|Av_1\|=\sqrt3$.
