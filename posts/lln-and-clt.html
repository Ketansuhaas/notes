Law of large numbers and the central limit theorem

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran

Builds on: Expectation and variance algebra, Concentration inequalities · Leads to: Sample variance and degrees of freedom, Confidence intervals

The sample mean is random

A different dataset gives a different $\bar x_n$. Its spread shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$, and that one fact drives both theorems below.

$$x_i\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}(\mu,\sigma^2), \qquad \bar x_n=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nx_i, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\bar x_n]=\mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar x_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}n.$$

Linearity for the mean; for the variance, independent terms' variances add and the $\tfrac1n$ comes out squared (variance algebra):

$$\mathbb{E}[\bar x_n]=\frac1n\sum_i\mu=\mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar x_n)=\frac1{n^2}\sum_i\sigma^2=\frac{\sigma^2}n.$$

Three kinds of convergence

Random sequences can "converge" in stronger or weaker senses.

$X_n\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}X$$P(\lim_nX_n=X)=1$each path settles
$X_n\xrightarrow{p}X$$P(|X_n-X|\gt\epsilon)\to0\ \ \forall\epsilon$big gaps get rare
$X_n\xrightarrow{d}X$$P(X_n\le x)\to P(X\le x)$ at every $x$ where the right side is continuousonly the histogram settles

$$\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\ \Rightarrow\ \xrightarrow{p}\ \Rightarrow\ \xrightarrow{d} \quad(\text{not the reverse}).$$

a.s. $\Rightarrow$ p (Proof idea). Let $B_n$ = "some $|X_m-X|\gt\epsilon$ with $m\ge n$". These events shrink as $n$ grows, and a path in all of them never settles, so their limit has probability $0$. Since $P(|X_n-X|\gt\epsilon)\le P(B_n)\to0$, done. (The limit step is continuity of $P$ along shrinking events.)

p $\Rightarrow$ d. If $X_n\le x$, then either $X\le x+\epsilon$ or $|X_n-X|\gt\epsilon$. Same idea the other way:

$$P(X_n\le x)\le P(X\le x+\epsilon)+P(|X_n-X|\gt\epsilon),$$

$$P(X_n\le x)\ge P(X\le x-\epsilon)-P(|X_n-X|\gt\epsilon).$$

Let $n\to\infty$ (the last terms vanish), then $\epsilon\to0$ at a continuity point: $P(X_n\le x)\to P(X\le x)$.

Not the reverse. $X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$ and $X_n=-X$: same distribution for every $n$ (symmetry), so $X_n\xrightarrow{d}X$, yet $|X_n-X|=2|X|$ never shrinks. For p without a.s.: independent $X_n\in\{0,1\}$ with $P(X_n=1)=\tfrac1n\to0$, but $\sum_n\tfrac1n=\infty$, so (second Borel-Cantelli lemma) $X_n=1$ infinitely often on almost every path.

Law of large numbers

Averaging cancels noise. With enough samples, $\bar x_n$ has nowhere to go but $\mu$.

Weak: $\bar x_n\xrightarrow{p}\mu$. Chebyshev on $\bar x_n$, whose variance is $\sigma^2/n$ (first section):

$$P(|\bar x_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\frac{\operatorname{Var}\bar x_n}{\epsilon^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0 \quad\text{for every }\epsilon\gt0.$$

That is the definition of $\xrightarrow{p}$.

$$\text{Strong: } \bar x_n\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\mu \quad(\text{needs only a finite mean}).$$

Proof idea (assuming a finite 4th moment). With $Y_i=x_i-\mu$, expanding $(\sum_iY_i)^4$, every term containing a lone $Y_i$ has mean $0$; only $Y_i^4$ ($n$ terms) and $Y_i^2Y_j^2$ ($3n(n-1)$ terms) survive. So $\mathbb{E}[(\sum_iY_i)^4]\le Kn^2$ for a constant $K$, and Markov on the 4th power gives

$$P(|\bar x_n-\mu|\ge\epsilon)\le\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\sum_iY_i)^4]}{n^4\epsilon^4}\le\frac{K}{n^2\epsilon^4}.$$

These sum to something finite over $n$, so (first Borel-Cantelli lemma) only finitely many $n$ have a deviation $\ge\epsilon$: the path settles. Needing only a finite mean (Etemadi) takes a truncation argument.

Example: fair coin, $\sigma^2=\tfrac14$

$\sigma^2=p(1-p)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12$ (Bernoulli). $n=10{,}000$, $\epsilon=0.05$:

$$P(|\bar x_n-0.5|\ge0.05)\le\dfrac{0.25}{10^4\cdot0.0025}=\frac{0.25}{25}=0.01.$$

Central limit theorem

LLN says where $\bar x_n$ ends up. CLT zooms in on the error at the $\sqrt n$ scale: it always looks Gaussian, whatever $p$ is (if $\sigma^2\lt\infty$).

$$\sqrt n\,(\bar x_n-\mu)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2) \quad\iff\quad Z_n=\frac{\bar x_n-\mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,1).$$

The two forms differ by the constant factor $\tfrac1\sigma$: $Z_n=\tfrac1\sigma\cdot\sqrt n(\bar x_n-\mu)$, and dividing a $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ by $\sigma$ gives variance $\sigma^2/\sigma^2=1$.

Proof idea: a sum of many small independent pieces. Write $Z_n=\sum_iY_i/(\sigma\sqrt n)$ with $Y_i=x_i-\mu$. Use $M(s)=\mathbb{E}e^{sY}$; independence turns the sum into a power:

$$\mathbb{E}\big[e^{sZ_n}\big]=\prod_i\mathbb{E}\big[e^{sY_i/(\sigma\sqrt n)}\big]=M\Big(\frac{s}{\sigma\sqrt n}\Big)^n.$$

Each piece is small, so Taylor-expand $M(u)=1+u\,\mathbb{E}Y+\tfrac{u^2}2\mathbb{E}Y^2+\dots=1+\tfrac{u^2\sigma^2}2+\dots$ (mean $0$, variance $\sigma^2$):

$$M\Big(\frac{s}{\sigma\sqrt n}\Big)^n=\Big(1+\frac{s^2}{2n}+o\big(\tfrac1n\big)\Big)^n\ \longrightarrow\ e^{s^2/2},$$

using $(1+\tfrac cn)^n\to e^c$. And $e^{s^2/2}$ is the MGF of $\mathcal N(0,1)$ (concentration note). Only the mean and variance of $Y$ survived the limit: that is why the shape of $p$ doesn't matter. A full proof uses characteristic functions $\mathbb{E}e^{isY}$ (which always exist, unlike the MGF) and Lévy's continuity theorem (converging transforms $\Rightarrow$ converging distributions).

So for large $n$: $\bar x_n=\mu+\tfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}Z_n$ with $Z_n\approx\mathcal N(0,1)$, and shifting and scaling a Gaussian keeps it Gaussian:

$$\bar x_n\approx\mathcal N\Big(\mu,\ \frac{\sigma^2}n\Big).$$

Example: fair coin, $n=100$

$\sigma/\sqrt n=0.5/10=0.05$, so $\bar x_n\approx\mathcal N(0.5,\ 0.05^2)$. A standard normal lands in $[-1.96,1.96]$ with probability $0.95$ (normal table), so $95\%$ of the time

$$\bar x_n\in0.5\pm1.96\cdot0.05=0.5\pm0.098\approx[0.40,\ 0.60].$$

Derive it yourself

Show that $\sqrt n$ is the only scaling $n^a$ for which $n^a(\bar x_n-\mu)$ has a nontrivial limit: compute its variance and see what happens for $a\lt\tfrac12$ and $a\gt\tfrac12$.

Solution

Scale rule, then $\operatorname{Var}\bar x_n=\sigma^2/n$:

$$\operatorname{Var}\big(n^a(\bar x_n-\mu)\big)=n^{2a}\cdot\frac{\sigma^2}n=n^{2a-1}\sigma^2.$$

$a\lt\tfrac12$: $2a-1\lt0$, so the variance $\to0$ and Chebyshev gives $P(|n^a(\bar x_n-\mu)|\ge\epsilon)\le n^{2a-1}\sigma^2/\epsilon^2\to0$. The limit is the constant $0$ (the error collapses to a point, the LLN view).

$a\gt\tfrac12$: write $n^a(\bar x_n-\mu)=n^{a-1/2}\cdot\sqrt n(\bar x_n-\mu)$. The second factor stays $\approx\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$ by the CLT, the first $\to\infty$, so for any fixed $M$

$$P\big(|n^a(\bar x_n-\mu)|\le M\big)=P\big(|\sqrt n(\bar x_n-\mu)|\le Mn^{1/2-a}\big)\to0:$$

all the mass escapes to infinity. Only $a=\tfrac12$ keeps variance $\sigma^2$ and a proper limit, the CLT scale.
