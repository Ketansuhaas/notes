A point has no length

Uncountably many points can't each carry positive mass: the total would be infinite. So measure sets by extent. A point has none.

Why the total blows up. Suppose uncountably many points had mass $\gt0$. Each has mass $\gt\frac1m$ for some $m=1,2,\dots$; if every one of these countably many groups were finite, the points would be countable. So some group, mass $\gt\frac1m$, is infinite. Take $N$ of its points:

$$\text{total}\ \ge\ N\cdot\frac1m\ \to\infty \quad (N\to\infty).$$

A point has length $0$. Length is $\lambda([a,b])=b-a$, and a bigger set has at least as much length (monotone). $\{x\}$ sits inside every $[x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon]$:

$$0\le\lambda(\{x\})\le\lambda([x-\varepsilon,x+\varepsilon])=2\varepsilon \ \text{ for all }\varepsilon\gt0 \ \Rightarrow\ \lambda(\{x\})=0.$$

$\lambda(\mathbb Q)=0$, though $\mathbb Q$ is dense. List the rationals $q_1,q_2,\dots$ (they are countable). Cover $q_k$ by an interval $I_k$ of length $\varepsilon/2^k$. The length of a countable union is at most the sum of lengths, so

$$\lambda(\mathbb Q)\le\sum_{k=1}^\infty\lambda(I_k)=\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{\varepsilon}{2^k}=\varepsilon\Big(\frac12+\frac14+\cdots\Big)=\varepsilon.$$

This holds for every $\varepsilon\gt0$, so $\lambda(\mathbb Q)=0$. (Equivalently, countable additivity: $\lambda(\mathbb Q)=\sum_k\lambda(\{q_k\})=\sum_k0=0$.)

Example: the Dirichlet function $D=\mathbb 1[x\in\mathbb Q]$

Lebesgue sorts by value: $D=1$ on $\mathbb Q$, $D=0$ on the rest, so

$$\int_0^1D\,d\lambda=1\cdot\underbrace{\lambda(\mathbb Q\cap[0,1])}_{0}+0\cdot\lambda([0,1]\setminus\mathbb Q)=0.$$

Riemann sorts by position and fails: every subinterval of any partition contains a rational and an irrational, so its sup is $1$ and its inf is $0$:

$$\text{upper sum}=\sum_i1\cdot\Delta x_i=1, \qquad \text{lower sum}=\sum_i0\cdot\Delta x_i=0, \ \text{ for every partition.}$$

Which sets count: a σ-algebra

No length works consistently on every subset of $\mathbb R$ (the Vitali set). So fix the eligible sets in advance: the events.

Proof idea (Vitali). Call $x\sim y$ in $[0,1]$ if $x-y\in\mathbb Q$, and let $V$ hold one point from each class (needs the axiom of choice). The shifts $V+r$, $r\in\mathbb Q\cap[-1,1]$, are disjoint and

$$[0,1]\subseteq\bigcup_r(V+r)\subseteq[-1,2].$$

If $V$ had a length $m$, shifting keeps it, and countable additivity gives $1\le\sum_rm\le3$. But a countably infinite sum of the same $m$ is $0$ (if $m=0$) or $\infty$ (if $m\gt0$). Contradiction, so $V$ gets no length.

$$\Omega\in\mathcal F, \qquad A\in\mathcal F\Rightarrow A^c\in\mathcal F, \qquad A_1,A_2,\ldots\in\mathcal F\Rightarrow\textstyle\bigcup_iA_i\in\mathcal F.$$

On $\mathbb R$: the Borel sets (generated by intervals). A probability space is $(\Omega,\mathcal F,P)$.

$P(X=x)=0$, yet $p(x)\gt0$

$P$ is mass, the density $p$ is a rate (mass per unit length). Zero width gives zero mass, whatever the rate.

$$P(A)=\int_Ap\,d\lambda, \qquad p=\frac{dP}{d\lambda}.$$

$p$ is a rate. For a continuous $p$, a short interval has $p\approx p(x)$ across it, so

$$P([x,x+h])=\int_x^{x+h}p\,d\lambda\approx p(x)\cdot h \quad\Rightarrow\quad p(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{P([x,x+h])}{h}.$$

A point gets $0$. $\{x\}\subseteq[x,x+h]$ for every $h$, so

$$P(X=x)\le P([x,x+h])\approx p(x)\,h\ \to0 \quad (h\to0).$$

(In general: an integral over a set of length $0$ is $0$, and $\lambda(\{x\})=0$.)

Example: $\mathcal N(0,0.01)$ at $0$

$\sigma^2=0.01$, $\sigma=0.1$, and the Gaussian density at its mean is $1/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}$:

$$p(0)=\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi\cdot0.01}}=\frac1{0.1\sqrt{2\pi}}=\frac{10}{2.507}\approx3.99\gt1.$$

Fine: it's a rate. Times a width gives a probability: $P(|X|\le0.05)\approx3.99\cdot0.1\approx0.40$ (the exact value, $P(|Z|\le0.5)\approx0.38$ from a normal table, is close).

One formula, two cases

Discrete and continuous differ only in the base measure $\mu$.

With counting measure, each point has $\mu(\{x\})=1$, so the integral is a sum of value times weight: $\int_Ap\,d\mu=\sum_{x\in A}p(x)\cdot1$.

$P(A)=\int_Ap\,d\mu$$\mu=$ counting ($\mu(\{x\})=1$)$\sum_{x\in A}p(x)$
$\mu=\lambda$ (length)$\int_Ap(x)\,dx$

KL for densities

Same definition as in the entropy note, with $\int\cdot\,d\mu$. Only the ratio $p/q$ enters, so KL stays $\ge0$ (Jensen) even though densities can exceed $1$.

$$\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=\int p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,d\mu(x).$$

Still $\ge0$. The same Jensen step as in the discrete case ($\log$ concave), now with $\int$:

$$-\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=\int p\log\frac qp\,d\mu\le\log\int p\,\frac qp\,d\mu$$

$$=\log\int_{p\gt0}q\,d\mu\le\log1=0.$$

Can be $\infty$. If $q=0$ on a set where $p\gt0$ (with $P$-mass $\gt0$), the integrand there is $p\log\frac p0=+\infty$.

Example: $p=\mathcal N(0,\sigma_1^2)$, $q=\mathcal N(0,\sigma_2^2)$ (nats)

Step 1: the log-ratio. The Gaussian density is $\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}$; the $\sqrt{2\pi}$ cancels:

$$\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}=\ln\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}-\frac{x^2}{2\sigma_1^2}+\frac{x^2}{2\sigma_2^2}.$$

Step 2: average under $p$, using $\mathbb{E}_p[x^2]=\sigma_1^2$ (mean $0$):

$$\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=\ln\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}-\frac{\sigma_1^2}{2\sigma_1^2}+\frac{\sigma_1^2}{2\sigma_2^2}=\ln\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}-\frac12+\frac{\sigma_1^2}{2\sigma_2^2}.$$

Step 3: plug in $\sigma_1=1,\ \sigma_2=2$, then swap roles for the reverse:

$\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)$$\ln2-\tfrac12+\tfrac{1}{8}=0.693-0.5+0.125=0.32$
$\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)$$\ln\tfrac12-\tfrac12+\tfrac{4}{2}=-0.693-0.5+2=0.81$

Not symmetric: a divergence, not a distance. (With different means too, the exercise adds a $\frac{(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2}$ term.)

Forward vs. reverse KL

The expectation is taken under the first argument, so that distribution decides where errors cost.

averaged underpunishesresult
Forward $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)$$p$$q\approx0$ where $p\gt0$mass-covering
Reverse $\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)$$q$$q\gt0$ where $p\approx0$mode-seeking

Proof idea: read off where each integrand is large.

$$\text{forward: } \int\underbrace{p}_{\text{weight}}\,\underbrace{\log\frac pq}_{\to\infty\text{ as }q\to0}\,d\mu, \qquad \text{reverse: } \int\underbrace{q}_{\text{weight}}\,\underbrace{\log\frac qp}_{\to\infty\text{ as }p\to0}\,d\mu.$$

Forward: wherever $p\gt0$, a tiny $q$ is heavily charged; where $p\approx0$ the weight is $0$, so extra $q$ mass there is free. So $q$ spreads over all of $p$. Reverse: wherever $q\gt0$, a tiny $p$ is heavily charged; where $q\approx0$ nothing is charged, so $q$ may ignore a mode of $p$ entirely.

Concretely, let $p=\tfrac12p_A+\tfrac12p_B$ with two far-apart modes, and $q=p_A$ (sit on one mode). On $q$'s support $p\approx\tfrac12p_A$, so

$$\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)\approx\int p_A\log\frac{p_A}{\tfrac12p_A}=\ln2\approx0.69 \ \text{(finite: reverse is happy)},$$

while forward KL puts weight $\tfrac12$ on mode $B$, where $q=p_A\approx0$, so $\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\to\infty$ as the modes separate.

The forward fit is moment matching. For Gaussian $q=\mathcal N(m,s^2)$, only the cross-entropy term depends on $q$:

$$\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=-H(p)+\mathbb{E}_p\Big[\tfrac12\ln(2\pi s^2)+\frac{(x-m)^2}{2s^2}\Big].$$

$$\partial_m:\ -\frac{\mathbb{E}_p[x-m]}{s^2}=0\ \Rightarrow\ m=\mathbb{E}_p[x],$$

$$\partial_{s^2}:\ \frac1{2s^2}-\frac{\mathbb{E}_p[(x-m)^2]}{2s^4}=0\ \Rightarrow\ s^2=\operatorname{Var}_p(x).$$

So the forward-KL Gaussian has the mean and variance of the whole two-mode $p$: centered between the modes and wide enough to cover both, as in the figure.

Forward KL is maximum likelihood (next note). Reverse KL drives variational inference (generative modeling).

Derive it yourself

Show $\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal N(\mu_1,\sigma^2)\,\|\,\mathcal N(\mu_2,\sigma^2)\big)=\dfrac{(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma^2}$.

Solution

Same $\sigma$, so the normalizers $\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}$ cancel in the ratio:

$$\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}=\frac{(x-\mu_2)^2-(x-\mu_1)^2}{2\sigma^2}.$$

Average under $p$. Write $x-\mu_2=(x-\mu_1)+(\mu_1-\mu_2)$; the cross term has $\mathbb{E}_p[x-\mu_1]=0$ (variance plus squared bias):

$$\mathbb{E}_p[(x-\mu_1)^2]=\sigma^2, \qquad \mathbb{E}_p[(x-\mu_2)^2]=\sigma^2+(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2.$$

$$\mathrm{KL}=\frac{\sigma^2+(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2-\sigma^2}{2\sigma^2}=\frac{(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma^2}.$$

Symmetric here, but only because the variances match. Example: $\mu_1=0,\ \mu_2=1,\ \sigma=1$ gives $\tfrac12$ nat.
