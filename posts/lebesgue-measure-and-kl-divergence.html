Lebesgue measure, densities, and KL divergence

Ketan Suhaas Saichandran · 30 October 2026
probabilitymeasure-theoryinformation-theory

Why measure theory at all

A probability measure $P$ assigns a number in $[0,1]$ to events (sets of outcomes), consistently: the measure of a countable disjoint union is the sum of the measures. On a discrete space, this is just bookkeeping — put a mass $p(x)\geq0$ on each point $x$, with $\sum_x p(x)=1$, and $P(A)=\sum_{x\in A}p(x)$.

On a continuous space like $\mathbb{R}$, that trick fails: there are uncountably many points, so if each carried positive mass the total would be infinite. The fix is to stop assigning mass to points and instead measure sets by their extent: the Lebesgue measure $\lambda$ gives an interval $[a,b]$ the measure $b-a$ (length), a region in $\mathbb{R}^2$ its area, and so on — it is the rigorous version of "length/area/volume," and it assigns every single point measure zero, consistent with the uncountability problem above.

Intuition: $\lambda$ is what lets you say "how big is this set" in a continuous space without ever asking "how much does this one point weigh," since the honest answer to the second question is always zero.

A probability measure $P$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is then built relative to $\lambda$ via a density $p(x)$, the rate at which $P$'s mass accumulates per unit of $\lambda$:

$$ P(A) = \int_A p(x)\,d\lambda(x) \qquad \Big(\text{write } d\lambda(x) = dx \text{ as usual}\Big). $$

This $p = \tfrac{dP}{d\lambda}$ is the Radon-Nikodym derivative of $P$ with respect to $\lambda$: whatever function you'd have to integrate against $\lambda$ to recover $P$ exactly. You will not need more machinery than that one sentence for anything in this series.

$P(X=x)=0$, yet $p(x)>0$

Probability notation and random variables flagged this tension and deferred it here; measure theory is exactly where it resolves. $p(x)$ is a density with respect to $\lambda$, not a probability: it is the local rate of $P$, not $P$'s value at a point. A single point has $\lambda$-measure zero, so $P(X=x)=\int_{\{x\}}p\,d\lambda=0$ regardless of how large $p(x)$ is — multiplying any finite height by zero width still gives zero. Meanwhile $p(x)$ itself can be large (it can exceed $1$, e.g. $\mathcal{N}(0,0.01)$ at $x=0$, from Common distributions), because it is measuring concentration, not mass.

Intuition: $P$ answers "how much mass sits on this set," $p$ answers "how fast would mass accumulate if you widened the set a little, right here." A point has no width to widen, so the first question always returns zero there, no matter what the second question returns.

One formula, two cases

Discrete and continuous are the same statement against two different reference (base) measures: $\lambda$ for continuous spaces, and the counting measure $\nu$ (mass $1$ on every point) for discrete ones. Against $\nu$, $P(A)=\int_A p\,d\nu=\sum_{x\in A}p(x)$ recovers the ordinary sum exactly. This is why expectation, KL divergence, and nearly every other formula below are written with a single $\int p(x)\,d\mu(x)$ and are read as $\sum_x p(x)(\cdots)$ whenever $x$ is discrete — the integral sign is not asserting continuity, it is just not committing to a particular base measure.

KL divergence

For two densities $p,q$ against the same base measure, the Kullback-Leibler divergence from $q$ to $p$ is:

$$ KL(p\|q) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\left[\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right] = \int p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,d\mu(x). $$

Non-negativity, $KL(p\|q)\geq0$ with equality iff $p=q$ (a.e.), follows from Jensen's inequality applied to the convex function $-\log$:

$$ KL(p\|q) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\left[-\log\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right] \geq -\log\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\left[\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right] = -\log\int q(x)\,d\mu(x) = -\log 1 = 0. $$

But $KL(p\|q)\neq KL(q\|p)$ in general, and KL satisfies no triangle inequality — it is a divergence, a measure of dissimilarity, not a distance.

Intuition: $\log(p/q)$ is large exactly where $p$ puts much more mass than $q$ does; averaging that under $p$ (not $q$) means places $p$ visits often are weighted heavily — and since the roles of $p,q$ aren't interchangeable in that average, neither is the result.

Forward vs. reverse KL

Because KL is asymmetric, $KL(p\|q)$ (forward, $p$ fixed/target, $q$ the approximation) and $KL(q\|p)$ (reverse) ask different questions and are minimized by different-looking $q$'s:

  • Forward, $KL(p\|q)$ — mode-covering. The average is taken under $p$, so any region where $p(x)>0$ but $q(x)\to0$ makes $\log(p/q)\to\infty$ there, and $p$ actually weights that region. $q$ is therefore punished severely for missing any mass of $p$, and is pushed to spread out and cover every mode $p$ has, even at the cost of also covering low-density regions in between.
  • Reverse, $KL(q\|p)$ — mode-seeking. The average is now under $q$, so $q$ only "pays" at points it actually visits; $q$ is free to ignore modes of $p$ entirely (if $q(x)\to0$ there, that term vanishes regardless of $p(x)$), but is punished for placing mass where $p(x)\to0$. $q$ therefore prefers to lock tightly onto a single mode of $p$ rather than spread thin across several.

Intuition: forward KL is afraid of zeros in $q$ (zero-avoiding → covers everything); reverse KL is afraid of its own mass landing where $p$ is zero (zero-forcing → hugs one mode and ignores the rest).

Intuition: forward KL is the divergence maximum likelihood estimation minimizes — the subject of Supervised learning as KL minimization next — while reverse KL (or objectives built from it) shows up in variational methods, part of the survey in Generative modeling.
